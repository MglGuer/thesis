\chapter{Results and Analysis}\label{chap:chapter6}

\section{Functional Testing}
  Functional testing is conducted to verify that each component of the Socius system behaves in accordance with its specified requirements and design.
This ensures that each feature produces the expected output. This testing phase will primarily concern itself with the User Interface (UI), Gemini API, Flask Backend,
and Supabase Database. The testing will be performed in a component-by-component manner to confirm that the end-to-end conversational pipeline/flow is working as intended.

  The testing strategy employed here will be primarily black-box testing (input and output examination from end-user side) at the component interfaces, supplemented by white-box 
inspection (reviewing the internal code and logic) of prompt engineering logic and JSON schema 
enforcement. Test cases are defined in terms of inputs, expected outputs, and pass/fail criteria for the testing to be objective. The Gemini API was accessed using the tier-1 paid plan
key associated with the research project, which imposes rate limits and may affect latency during sustained testing sequences.

\subsection{Testing Scope and Approach}
The scope of functional testing for Socius encompasses the following seven testing areas, each corresponding to a major architectural component:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{User Interface (UI) Testing:} verification of user-facing features including chat window rendering, scenario selection, and profile display.
  \item \textbf{Session and Authentication Testing:} verification of user profile initialization, session isolation, and login/registration flows.
  \item \textbf{Input Processing Testing:} verification of the intent recognition algorithm, entity extraction, out-of-scope detection, and typographic correction.
  \item \textbf{Conversation Flow Manager Testing: } verification of dialogue state tracking, scenario instantiation, persona adoption, and turn-taking logic.
  \item \textbf{Pragmatic Analyzer Testing:} verification of maxim violation detection, implicature scoring, JSON Schema Controlled Generation, and quantitative metric output.
  \item \textbf{Feedback Generation Testing:} verification of post-session feedback quality, AI Mentor persona consistency, and Holistic Mentor longitudinal aggregation.
  \item \textbf{Database Manager Testing:} verification of correct data storage and retrieval for User Profiles, Conversation Logs, and Scenarios.
\end{itemize}

These test cases are stored on a google sheet in the following format: Test Case ID, Test Category, Test Description, Test Input, Expected Output, Pass/Fail Result. The testing
is conducted by the research proponents on the deployed development instance of the system. For test cases containing LLM output and subjective evaluation, a minimum of 2 proponents are 
required for inter-rater reliability. Moreover, the prompt-engineering utilized to develop the system will only guide Gemini-3 Flash for responses and the system's outputs are inherently non-deterministic.
Therefore, the functional tests for LLM-dependent components evaluate structure and format rather than exact content unless the test may probe safety configuration.

\section{Expert Testing}
Prior to end-user testing, the 10 conversational scenarios and their underlying theoretical framework were submitted for expert review to ensure their validity (as instruments for pragmatic competence training) and relevance to the target demographic. The evaluation was conducted through online means,
wherein the research proponents sent a structured evaluation sheet containing each document scenario with eleven dimensions alongside a shortened research paper. The dimensions are the following:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Title
  \item Recommended Age Range
  \item Thematic Calssification (e.g., Identity vs.\ Expectation, the 4 themes of interpersonal conflict)
  \item Corresponding Havighurst Developmental Task
  \item User Role
  \item Chatbot Role
  \item Pragmatic Conflict Description
  \item Primary Gricean Maxims Implicated
  \item Filipino Cultural Values
  \item Expected Pragmatic Skill
\end{itemize}

This documentation allows the expert to view the scenarios in its full breadth and depth, which is necessary for a comprehensive evaluation of the scenario's design and alignment to the research's theoretical frameworks. The expert was given a 3-point Likert scale (0 = Not Suitable, 1 = Needs Revision, 2 = Suitable) to rate 6 specific dimensions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Grice's Maxims Alignment (Quantity, Quality, Relation, Manner)
  \item Relevance to Young Adult Contexts 
  \item Cultural Appropriateness 
  \item Learning Value 
  \item Complexity and Progression 
  \item Clarity and Structure 
\end{itemize}

The research proponents also facilitated an online meeting to formally introduce the proposed frameworks in the research, to break down the structure of our research and scenario design, and to clarify any questions that the expert may have had. The expert's ratings and qualitative feedback were then compiled and analyzed to identify any necessary revisions to the list. 
Considering this, the comments from the expert were generally positive and that the frameworks were valid. The expert also provided suggestions to change certain details in a scenario that would make the scenario feel more controlled (e.g. keeping a scenario one-on-one rather than having a group approach, changing chatbot role to be more relatable to a student, etc.) as it may deter from the scenario's intended pragmatic conflict.
These suggestions were then implemented and clarified on the face-to-face meeting with the expert, where we let the expert demo the system and experience the scenarios first-hand. The expert had then fully validated and approved the frameworks that went into training pragmatical competence.

\subsection{\colorbox{yellow}{End-User Testing}}
 
End-user testing was conducted to evaluate Socius from the perspective of its intended target demographic,
 examining its usability, perceived educational value, and experiential quality across a multi-day testing 
 program. Each participant was asked to complete at least one conversation session per day and answer a 
 post-interaction survey after each session. A consolidated end-of-program survey was administered at the 
 conclusion of testing to capture extended impressions of the system.
 
\subsubsection{Participant Demographics}
 
A purposive convenience sample of $N = 24$ participants completed conversation sessions in Socius,
 generating a total of 116 valid session logs after excluding developer accounts and sessions where 
 the transcript contained no user input. Participants were all young adults within the 18--24 age 
 range, consistent with the system's target demographic. The sample was predominantly composed of 
 undergraduate students from De La Salle University and its associated campuses, with additional 
 participants from the University of the Philippines Los Ba\~{n}os, Holy Angel University, and Ateneo 
 de Davao University. Disciplines represented included Computer Science, Psychology, Marketing 
 Management, Accountancy, Mathematics, Organizational and Social Systems Development, and Communication 
 and Media Studies, among others.
 
To coordinate testing and provide procedural guidance without influencing conversational behavior,
 a Discord server was established as the communication channel between the research team and participants. 
 Interaction with the Socius chatbot itself was conducted independently by each participant at their own 
 discretion. Prior to the testing program, a Pre-Assessment Survey was administered, and a Consolidated 
 End-of-Program Survey was collected from participants upon program completion.

\subsubsection{Qualitative Feedback Analysis}
 
Open-ended responses from both the post-interaction surveys and the consolidated end-of-program survey were 
reviewed thematically. Six recurring themes emerged across the qualitative data corpus.
 
\paragraph{Ecological Validity of Scenarios.} Participants repeatedly noted that the scenarios closely 
mirrored interpersonal situations they had personally encountered or anticipated facing. Several participants 
described emotional responses consistent with high ecological validity, including one who described the 
curfew scenario as hitting (P14) ``too well that it's kind of eerie,'' another who reported being (P24) ``genuinely scared 
when the bot got upset,'' and a third who reflected that the political dinner scenario (P04) ``can be a bit anxiety 
driving since some scenarios are high in conflict.'' The AI Partner's ability to maintain contextually specific 
Taglish dialogue and character-consistent emotional escalation was a primary driver of immersion.
 
\paragraph{Cultural Localization as a Distinctive Feature.} The integration of Filipino cultural 
frameworks, namely \textit{Hiya}, \textit{Pakikisama}, \textit{Utang na Loob}, \textit{Paggalang}, and 
\textit{Kapwa}, was cited by multiple participants as the most distinctive and valued feature of 
Socius relative to generic chatbot experiences. Participants noted that the system (P04) ``placed conversations 
in the Philippine context and emphasized cultural aspects to help users understand better why Filipinos 
can act the way they do.'' The AI Mentor's use of Taglish during the debrief phase was specifically 
appreciated for making feedback feel accessible and relational rather than instructional and foreign.
 
\paragraph{AI Mentor Feedback Format.} While the substance of the AI Mentor's feedback was well-received, 
a recurring concern was the density and delivery format of feedback text. Participants described the mentor 
as producing (P15) ``a block of text'' that was not conducive to active engagement. One participant explicitly 
recommended a more dialogic feedback modality in which the mentor asks reflective questions (e.g., ``Where 
do you think you went wrong?'') rather than front-loading a comprehensive analytical debrief. This suggestion 
aligns with established principles of conversational formative assessment and represents a substantive future 
design direction.
 
\paragraph{Repetitive Dialogue Patterns.} Multiple participants independently identified a tendency for the 
AI Partner to re-anchor the same scenario trigger verbatim across successive turns. For instance, repeatedly 
referencing a specific physical incident in the relationship scenario across nearly every message. This 
behavior was perceived as unnatural and disrupted immersion. The pattern suggests a context management 
issue within the Conversation Flow Manager's handling of scenario-critical anchors, wherein the model 
over-weighted early scenario premises rather than tracking the evolving conversational state.
 
\paragraph{Role Reversal Error.} At least two participants independently reported a session in the 
\textit{``Wala Naman Akong Pera'' Friend} scenario (Scenario 6) where the AI Partner appeared to reverse 
roles mid-conversation, suddenly framing the user as the indebted party rather than the creditor being 
solicited. This constitutes a confirmed persona anchoring error in the CFM's role management logic. The 
error appears to have been triggered by linguistically ambiguous user responses (e.g., ``Sorry, medyo kapos 
narin ako ngayon''), which the model interpreted as a role-symmetrical admission of financial constraint 
rather than a refusal. This "bug" was identified by the research team for remediation in subsequent iterations.
 
\paragraph{Psychological Safety and Disinhibition.} Participants across multiple survey entries noted feeling 
able to communicate more candidly in Socius than in real interpersonal contexts. One participant stated that 
(P19) ``because it is a robot, I can say whatever I want without repercussion,'' and another described Socius as 
enabling scenario practice (P02) ``without the apprehension of how the other person feels.'' This low-stakes 
disinhibition effect, a theoretically anticipated advantage of AI-mediated pragmatic training environments, 
appears to have been operationalised successfully through the system's design, producing the conditions 
under which learners are willing to attempt pragmatic strategies they might otherwise avoid in authentic 
high-stakes interactions.
