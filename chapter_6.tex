\chapter{Results and Analysis}\label{chap:chapter6}

\section{Functional Testing}
  Functional testing is conducted to verify that each component of the Socius system behaves in accordance with its specified requirements and design.
This ensures that each feature produces the expected output. This testing phase will primarily concern itself with the User Interface (UI), Gemini API, Flask Backend,
and Supabase Database. The testing will be performed in a component-by-component manner to confirm that the end-to-end conversational pipeline/flow is working as intended.
 
  The testing strategy employed here will be primarily black-box testing (input and output examination from end-user side) at the component interfaces, supplemented by white-box 
inspection (reviewing the internal code and logic) of prompt engineering logic and JSON schema 
enforcement. Test cases are defined in terms of inputs, expected outputs, and pass/fail criteria for the testing to be objective. The Gemini API was accessed using the tier-1 paid plan
key associated with the research project, which imposes rate limits and may affect latency during sustained testing sequences.
 
\subsection{Testing Scope and Approach}
The scope of functional testing for Socius encompasses the following seven testing areas, each corresponding to a major architectural component:
 
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{User Interface (UI) Testing:} verification of user-facing features including chat window rendering, scenario selection, and profile display.
  \item \textbf{Session and Authentication Testing:} verification of user profile initialization, session isolation, and login/registration flows.
  \item \textbf{Input Processing Testing:} verification of the intent recognition algorithm, entity extraction, out-of-scope detection, and typographic correction.
  \item \textbf{Conversation Flow Manager Testing: } verification of dialogue state tracking, scenario instantiation, persona adoption, and turn-taking logic.
  \item \textbf{Pragmatic Analyzer Testing:} verification of maxim violation detection, implicature scoring, JSON Schema Controlled Generation, and quantitative metric output.
  \item \textbf{Feedback Generation Testing:} verification of post-session feedback quality, AI Mentor persona consistency, and Holistic Mentor longitudinal aggregation.
  \item \textbf{Database Manager Testing:} verification of correct data storage and retrieval for User Profiles, Conversation Logs, and Scenarios.
\end{itemize}
 
These test cases are stored on a google sheet in the following format: Test Case ID, Test Category, Test Description, Test Input, Expected Output, Pass/Fail Result, and stress test results. The testing
was conducted on the deployed development instance of the system. For test cases containing LLM output and subjective evaluation, a minimum of 2 research proponents are 
required for inter-rater reliability considering the lack of expert knowledge. Moreover, the prompt-engineering utilized to develop the system will only guide Gemini-3 Flash for responses and the system's outputs are inherently non-deterministic.
Therefore, the functional tests for LLM-dependent components evaluate structure and format rather than exact content unless the test may probe safety configuration. The test cases will also undergo
stress testing, which involves repeated execution to evaluate consistency and identify any intermittent failures. 15 stress test runs are conducted for components that don't rely on LLM output, rather focusing
on the functionality of said component. Those test cases that are evaluated at 55 test runs are ones that are LLM- and situation-dependent.

\subsection{\colorbox{yellow}{End-User Testing}}
 
End-user testing was conducted to evaluate Socius from the perspective of its intended target demographic,
 examining its usability, perceived educational value, and experiential quality across a multi-day testing 
 program. Each participant was asked to complete at least one conversation session per day and answer a 
 post-interaction survey after each session. A consolidated end-of-program survey was administered at the 
 conclusion of testing to capture extended impressions of the system.
 
\subsubsection{Participant Demographics}
 
A purposive convenience sample of $N = 24$ participants completed conversation sessions in Socius,
 generating a total of 116 valid session logs after excluding developer accounts and sessions where 
 the transcript contained no user input. Participants were all young adults within the 18--24 age 
 range, consistent with the system's target demographic. The sample was predominantly composed of 
 undergraduate students from De La Salle University and its associated campuses, with additional 
 participants from the University of the Philippines Los Ba\~{n}os, Holy Angel University, and Ateneo 
 de Davao University. Disciplines represented included Computer Science, Psychology, Marketing 
 Management, Accountancy, Mathematics, Organizational and Social Systems Development, and Communication 
 and Media Studies, among others.
 
To coordinate testing and provide procedural guidance without influencing conversational behavior,
 a Discord server was established as the communication channel between the research team and participants. 
 Interaction with the Socius chatbot itself was conducted independently by each participant at their own 
 discretion. Prior to the testing program, a Pre-Assessment Survey was administered, and a Consolidated 
 End-of-Program Survey was collected from participants upon program completion.

using DeVito's (2017) communication noise taxonomy as applied to human-to-agent interaction contexts by \citeA{ureta2025}. 
These are types of noises identified by DeVito that interfere with interlocutors' abilities to send and receive messages, which thereby cause communcation
breakdown. These include technological noise from the computing devices used as the medium of communication; semantic noise due to language gaps and communication
ambiguity of certain words; psychological noise arising from biases, preconceived ideas, and lack of investment in the interaction; and environmental noise that relate
to the broader social and cultural context of the interaction. As such, the 6 themes applied with this framework are the following:}
 
\noindent\textbf{\hl{Technological Noise: Repetitive Dialogue Patterns}}

\hl{Multiple participants independently identified a tendency for the 
AI Partner to re-anchor the same scenario trigger verbatim across successive turns. For instance, repeatedly 
referencing a specific physical incident in the relationship scenario across nearly every message. This 
behavior was perceived as unnatural and disrupted immersion. The pattern suggests a form of technological and context management 
issue within the Conversation Flow Manager's handling of scenario-critical anchors, wherein the model 
over-weighted early scenario premises rather than tracking the evolving conversational state. As \citeA{ureta2025} note, 
technology-based communication channels can cause breakdowns when messages are 
not properly processed through the communication pipeline, and this pattern 
exemplifies that dynamic at the model-architecture level.
}

\noindent\textbf{\hl{Technological Noise: Emergent Manipulative Behaviour in the AI Partner}}

\hl{
At least two participants independently reported a session in the 
\textit{``Wala Naman Akong Pera'' Friend} scenario (Scenario~6) where the AI 
Partner appeared to reverse the power dynamic mid-conversation, suddenly framing 
the user as the indebted or negligent party rather than the creditor being 
solicited. Initial analysis attributed this to a persona anchoring error 
triggered by linguistically ambiguous user responses (e.g., 
\textit{``Sorry, medyo kapos narin ako ngayon''}). However, closer analysis revealed that as the
user continued to confront the AI partner's understanding and role in the scenario, the AI partner seemingly
acknowledged the role but continued to deflect responsibility and still try to leverage the user in the conversation. 
It had genuinely tried to invoke guilt, minimise the user's concerns, and regrame the user's hesitation on "paying their debt" 
as a personal failing. Thus, it had utilized conversational tactics rather than misidentifying its role. This behaviorappears to have been triggered by linguistically ambiguous user responses (e.g., ``Sorry, medyo kapos 
narin ako ngayon''), which the model interpreted as a role-symmetrical admission of financial constraint 
rather than a refusal. This consitutes an instance of technological noise that is qualitatively disting due to it not being a system issue of socius, but rather an 
emergent behavior of the LLM's conversational capabilities for persuasive and socially adversarial language generation. This can be framed as the 
prompt's failure to sufficiently constrain the model's generation space, yet this had also then resonated with the users lived experiences or real life scenarios.
Thus, the model had extrapolated from the prompt's scenario description to manipulative pressure tactics that are arguably realistic. Thus, this emergent behavior
represent a problem of mixing realism into a scenario. A user that would internalise the AI partner's framing of a situation that requires them to resist rather than accept
blame would risk the user drawing incorrect conclusions from their own pragmatic performance. This highlights a risk in affective persona engineering. The more faithfully an LLM adopts the psychology of a difficult interlocutor, the more carefully must the system constrain the boundaries
between realistic social pressure and harmful manipulation.
}

\noindent\textbf{Semantic Noise: AI Mentor Feedback Format}

While the substance of the AI Mentor's feedback was well-received, 
a recurring concern was the density and delivery format of feedback text. Participants described the mentor 
as producing (P15) ``a block of text'' that was not conducive to active engagement. One participant explicitly 
recommended a more dialogic feedback modality in which the mentor asks reflective questions (e.g., ``Where 
do you think you went wrong?'') rather than front-loading a comprehensive analytical debrief. \hl{This reflects semantic noise arising from a 
register mismatch between the system's analytical output language and users' expectations of a conversational reflective exchange. This exhibits symptoms (vocabulary gaps, comprehension style of receiver
communicative mode of sender (AI Partner)) that are consistent with the defined semantic noise.} This suggestion 
aligns with established principles of conversational formative assessment and represents a substantive future 
design direction.

\noindent\textbf{\hl{Psychological Noise: Emotional Engagement in High-Conflict Scenarios}}

\hl{Participants repeatedly noted that the scenarios closely mirrored interpersonal 
situations they had personally encountered or anticipated facing. Several participants 
described emotional responses consistent with high ecological validity, including one 
who described the curfew scenario as hitting (P14) ``too well that it's kind of eerie,'' 
another who reported being (P24) ``genuinely scared when the bot got upset,'' and a 
third who reflected that the political dinner scenario (P04) ``can be a bit anxiety 
driving since some scenarios are high in conflict.'' The AI Partner's ability to 
maintain contextually specific Taglish dialogue and character-consistent emotional 
escalation was a primary driver of immersion. In DeVito's (2017) taxonomy, 
psychological noise refers to the cognitive and affective states that interfere with 
the processing of communicative content. The trade-off of having ecological validity 
that invokes authentic emotional engagement embedded into each scenario also risks user's
affective states and capacity to deliberate pragmatic reflection mid-interaction.
suggests that the difficulty gradient of scenarios may benefit from being paired 
with in-session scaffolding mechanisms that help users regulate emotions without 
breaking immersion.}

\noindent\textbf{\hl{Environmental Noise Mitigation: Cultural Localization as a 
Protective Factor.}}

\hl{The integration of Filipino cultural frameworks, namely \textit{Hiya}, 
\textit{Pakikisama}, \textit{Utang na Loob}, \textit{Paggalang}, and 
\textit{Kapwa}, was cited by multiple participants as the most distinctive and 
valued feature of Socius relative to generic chatbot experiences. Participants noted 
that the system (P04) ``placed conversations in the Philippine context and emphasized 
cultural aspects to help users understand better why Filipinos can act the way they 
do.'' The AI Mentor's use of Taglish during the debrief phase was specifically 
appreciated for making feedback feel accessible and relational rather than 
instructional and foreign. Environmental noise, as described by DeVito (2017), 
encompasses the broader social and cultural context that influences the perception of 
legibility and relatability to the receiver. As such, the deliberate cultural localization of Socius's scenario design and feedback 
language functioned as a noise-reduction mechanism as the environment within Socius and the user's lived experiences
align a common ground. This alignment had the effect of reducing cognitive load and making the user feel
comfortable and familiar with the given scenarios, which thereby allowed them more to focus on pragmatic learning.}

\noindent\textbf{\hl{Psychological Noise Mitigation: Psychological Safety and Disinhibition}}

\hl{Survey responses frequently highlighted the benefit of practicing in a low-stakes environment. Participants noted that they felt more comfortable making mistakes with Socius than they would in real-life interactions. For example, Participant 19 mentioned feeling free to ``say whatever I want without repercussion,'' while Participant 02 appreciated practicing ``without the apprehension of how the other person feels.''

These comments suggest that the chatbot successfully provided a psychologically safe space for users. By removing the fear of social judgment, the system encouraged learners to test out new conversational strategies and navigate difficult dialogues that they might otherwise avoid in authentic, high-stakes interactions.}


\subsection{\hl{Findings vis-à-vis Research Objectives}}

This section reflects on the extent to which the results of functional testing, 
expert validation, and end-user testing speak to each of the four research 
objectives stated in Chapter 1.

\paragraph{Objective 1 (Dual-Agent Architecture):} 

The first objective was to design and implement a dual-agent architecture 
utilizing the Google Gemini API to facilitate a natural roleplay experience 
and deep pragmatic analysis. The results of end-to-end integration testing 
provide strong evidence that this objective was achieved at the technical 
level. All six TC-INT cases passed across all 15 stress test runs, confirming 
that the full APAM pipeline functioned reliably. The mode-switch between the AI Partner 
and AI Mentor configurations (TC-INT-04) was reliable across all repetitions, 
and the multi-instance Gemini configuration functioned as a coordinated system.

The qualitative data from end-user testing 
also revealed a meaningful gap between the architecture's functional correctness 
and the naturalism of the experience it produces. Participants reported that 
AI Partner responses were frequently too long and repetitive. This was due to the format of the text and response itself being in message format,
which is not optimized for natural feeling conversation flow that would occur in real life. These findings suggest that while the 
dual-agent architecture is sound as an infrastructure, delivery format parameters require further refinement to meet the 
intended standard of naturalistic roleplay.

\paragraph{Objective 2 (Theoretical Framework):} 

The second objective was to formulate a theoretical framework for scenario 
generation synthesizing Grice's Cooperative Principle, Havighurst's Developmental Tasks, Arnett's Theory of Emerging Adulthood, 
and Filipino Cultural Values. Expert validation 
confirmed that the framework is structurally sound and culturally nuanced. 
The expert awarded a rating of 2 (Suitable) across all six evaluation criteria 
for eight of the ten evaluated scenarios, with only Scenario~3 requiring 
revision before approval.

The quantitative data also provides further context. Scenario~2 (\textit{The ``Pauwi Na'' Curfew}), 
anchored in the developmental task of achieving emotional independence and 
grounded in the universally familiar \textit{Paggalang} script, produced the 
highest scores across all three dimensions ($M_{\text{IC}} = 4.77$, 
$M_{\text{SN}} = 4.47$, $M_{\text{CE}} = 4.64$). By contrast, Scenario~9 
(\textit{The Micromanaging Boss}), which requires simultaneous formal deference 
and competence assertion within a hierarchical professional power differential, 
produced the lowest CE and SN scores ($M_{\text{CE}} = 2.78$, 
$M_{\text{SN}} = 2.75$). Arguably, these two are of differing difficulty levels, considering that 
Scenario 2 requires the user to navigate a lived experience that is culturally familiar (considering also that the sample is mostly
College students studying in Metro Manila) while scenario 9 requires the user to navigate a more complex power dynamic that is less commonly encounter in the
users' lived experience. Thus, this pattern supports the intended difficulty gradient rooted in the Havighurst-Arnett conflict alignment and developmental task. 
It can be assumed that the scenarios are operationalizing the theoretical framework it is based on.

\paragraph{Objective 3 (Scoring Rubric):} 

The third objective was to develop a multi-level, quantitative scoring rubric 
for pragmatic competence enforced via JSON Schema Controlled Generation, 
providing detailed and actionable metrics across Intention Coherence, Social 
Affective Nuance, and Communicative Effectiveness. At the structural and 
technical levels, this objective was achieved. TC-PA-01 and TC-PA-02 both 
passed across all 15 stress test runs, confirming that the JSON Schema 
enforcement reliably produces complete, range-valid output across all 8 
criteria. Expert validation confirmed that the whole scoring rubric 
constitutes a valid and grounded approach to quantifying pragmatic 
ability for the purposes of this research.

Nevertheless, the results surface two important limitations regarding the 
rubric's reliability in functional testing. First, scoring non-determinism was observed 
in TC-PA-05, which passed only 10 out of 15 runs. This indicates that the model's 
interpretation of identical ambiguous utterances varies across executions. 
Second, the Holistic Mentor's scenario recommendation logic, evaluated in 
TC-FG-06, passed only 7 out of 15 runs, which is the lowest stress test pass rate. 
This suggests that the AI Mentor's analysis and longitudinal feedback layer is insufficiently constrained to produce consistent outputs.
In conjunction, these findings indicate that the outputs of the scoring rubric are not reliable enough to be treated as
objective measures of pragmatic ability and psychometric measures without concurrent human validation. 


\paragraph{Objective 4 (Evaluate Effectiveness):} 

The fourth objective was to evaluate the chatbot's effectiveness in enhancing 
users' pragmatic competence and social communication skills through user 
feedback and human user testing. The results present a nuanced picture that 
must be interpreted carefully against the constraints of the evaluation design.

Quantitatively, the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test identified one statistically 
significant improvement across the Pre-Post window: SN Formality Level 
($W = 58.5$, $p = .047$, $r = .424$, medium effect). This indicates that 
participants showed a reliable positive shift in their calibration of register 
appropriateness between their first and final sessions. The remaining metrics did not reach 
significance at the $\alpha = .05$ threshold despite trending positive for IC and SN. This pattern is consistent with 
established findings in pragmatic competence research which indicates that 
surface-level form features are more responsive to short-term feedback than deeper social inference skills 
\cite{house1996, kasper1996}. Thus, the short testing duration of approximately 
five sessions per participant per day is likely the primary factor for the absence of significant gains across the metrics.

Qualitatively, the survey data reflects a favorable user perception of the 
system's educational impact. Participants reported increased confidence in 
their social communication skills ($M = 4.06$), belief in the system's 
long-term benefit ($M = 4.31$), and strong intent to reuse ($M = 4.62$). 
These results suggest that the system the psychological and motivational conditions for a learning environment and it is
required to use this prerequisite for testing of longer-term learning outcomes designed for the system to support.


\subsection{\hl{AI System Risks and Warnings}}

The use of a probabilistic LLM as the core engine of a pedagogical system introduces a set of risks that must be transparently acknowledged. These are not
hypothetical concers, rather these are observed phenomena encountered during functional and end-user testing which carry implications for how the system's outputs
should be intepreted by users and educators. These risks include:

\subsubsection{Scoring Validity and the Risk of Misplaced Trust}

The pragmatic competence scores generated by the AI mentor are produced by a probabilistic model, even more so that the responses of the AI partner during the conversation transcript
also very across multiple runs. This means that the scores are LLM-reliant and are measures decided by the model's interpretation of the user's performance. As such, thesear
e not rated against human expert judgment. Despite inter-rater testing, the production of scores, at Socius' current iteration, is still non-deterministic and can vary across runs. Users and educators who encounter 
these scores should therefore treat them as indicative rather than definitive measures of pragmatic ability. This results in a risk of misplaced trust if particularly acute in contexts where scores are used
to make consequential decisions about a user's learning progress regarding pragmatic competence. The balance between faithful psychological register of the AI Partner and system safety constraints for persona boundary must be assessed. 
There is a need for a systematic human validation study to establish the reliability of the scoring rubric, 
and as such the outputs of the scoring rubric should be positioned as a formative feedback tool rather than a summative assessment measure.


\subsubsection{Emergent Manipulative Behaviour and Pedagogical Harm}

The AI Partner in Scenario 3 and 6 exhibited emergent manipulative behavior such as invoking guilt, deflecting 
responsibility, and reframing the user's pragmatically appropriate refusal as a personal failing. This behavior arose from
the LLM's capacity to generate socially adversarial language that is then consistent and natural to the scenario's description. It depicts realism
in the scenario, but it also risks users internalizing the AI Partner's framing of a situation to be one that requires them to just accept blame rather than resist it, as described earlier.
This may lead the users to drawing incorrect conclusions about their own communicative performance. This highlights a risk in balancing realism and pedagogical safety in the engineering
of a chatbot role/persona in the roleplay scenario. Thus, explicit boundaries must be established and adversarial output testing must be further expanded to ensure that the
AI Partner's behavior remains within the intended pedagogical scope and does not inadvertently model harmful interaction patterns.

\subsubsection{Hallucination and the Fabrication of Pragmatic Feedback}

A well-documented risk of LLM-based systems is the tendency to generate 
plausible-sounding but factually incorrect or unsupported outputs, commonly 
referred to as hallucination \cite{ji_survey_2023}. In Socius, the AI Mentor may generate feedback that 
references pragmatic violations, cultural value applications, or specific 
utterances from the conversation transcript that are inaccurate, 
misattributed, or fabricated. Because the AI Mentor's feedback is delivered 
with a consistent, constructive tone, users have no reliable signal to distinguish between accurate and hallucinated 
feedback. A user who receives confidently stated but incorrect analysis of 
their pragmatic performance may internalize a distorted understanding of 
their own communicative behavior. This is particularly concerning in the 
context of cultural value feedback: the AI Mentor's assessments of whether 
a user appropriately applied \textit{Hiya}, \textit{Pakikisama}, or 
\textit{Paggalang} are not verified against any ground truth and rely 
entirely on the model's probabilistic interpretation of the transcript. This problem also
stems from the fact that the testing phase did not include a mandatory end-user test and expert validation of the AI Mentor's feedback, of which the expert
, who had a hand's on experience of the system, had noted that the feedback was only acceptable. There was no systematic validation of the feedback's quality and accuracy. 
There is currently no mechanism in the system to flag uncertain assessments nor to alert users when feedback may not 
accurately reflect the conversation. This represents a risk to 
the validity of the learning experience and underscores the need for human expert review of 
AI-generated pragmatic feedback before the system is used in any consequential 
educational context.

\subsubsection{Bias in LLM-Generated Pragmatic Assessment}

There is risk of systematic bias in how the AI Mentor evaluates 
pragmatic performance due to how LLMs are trained on multitudes and large corpora of data \cite{bender_dangers_2021}. The model's implicit standard of ``appropriate'' 
formality, ``effective'' implicature handling, or ``culturally sensitive'' 
behavior may not accurately reflect Filipino communicative norms despite prompt-engineer efforts. The model's 
evaluation of \textit{Hiya}-compliant indirectness, for example, may be 
calibrated against Western pragmatic norms in which directness is more 
valued, leading to systematic underscoring of users who communicate in ways 
that are culturally appropriate but unfamiliar to the model's training 
distribution. The Social Affective Nuance category, which produced the 
lowest grand mean across all sessions ($M = 3.56$), and the Cultural 
Filipino Values sub-score, which was the lowest-scoring sub-dimension at 
baseline ($M_{\text{Pre}} = 2.977$), may partly reflect this bias rather 
than users lacking an understanding of the concepts themselves. Without a 
comparative study in which human pragmatics experts score the same 
transcripts using the same rubric, it is not possible to determine the 
extent to which the AI Mentor's assessments reflect learner performance 
versus model bias.

\section{\hl{System Reliability and Technical Anomalies}}

While Socius demonstrated strong pedagogical potential, post-intervention surveys and system logs revealed that technical and architectural anomalies impacted the user experience. According to the quantitative Likert distributions from the consolidated feedback, a significant majority of participants (75\%) disagreed or strongly disagreed with the statement that the system was perfectly technically reliable throughout the week.

To understand the nature of these friction points, a qualitative analysis of the open-ended feedback and the database's \texttt{conversation\_logs} was conducted. The anomalies encountered by users were categorized into three primary bug types of varying severities.

\subsection{\hl{Persona Bleed and Role Reversal (High Severity)}}
The most disruptive anomaly was the Large Language Model's (LLM) tendency to hallucinate role boundaries, specifically in high-conflict financial scenarios. Multiple participants (e.g., P20, P22, P25) reported that the AI Partner would spontaneously reverse the scenario's premise midway through the conversation. 

For instance, in the \textit{``The `Wala Naman Akong Pera' Friend''} scenario---where the AI is programmed as the debtor---the model occasionally suffered from context-window confusion and accused the user of owing money instead.

\begin{quote}
\textit{``It turned the situation around with me as the one who borrowed money and couldn't pay back, which left me confused... even as I tried to end the conversation with `Sige bes. Thank you', it just kind of reiterated the last message.''} \textbf{-- P25 (Day 1)}
\end{quote}

A review of the system transcripts corroborates this hallucination. As seen in the log excerpt below, the AI generated an out-of-context response, entirely flipping the financial burden onto the user:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={\hl{Log Excerpt: Persona Bleed in Scenario 1 (Captured from P06, Day 3)}}, label={lst:persona_bleed}]
Partner: Hala, sige bes, gets ko naman. Pero... pasensya na talaga ha... Akala ko okay na yung budget mo kasi nakabili ka nun. Tapos ngayon, pati pambayad ng kuryente kukulangin? Alam mo naman na may utang ka pa sa akin nung nakaraang buwan, 'di ba?
\end{lstlisting}

This bug severely broke immersion. As P16 noted in the consolidated feedback, when the system fails to maintain the logic of the situation, \textit{``it doesn't really train for an actual situation and also removes the immersion.''}

\subsection{\hl{Network Latency and Premature Scoring (Moderate Severity)}}
Because the AI Mentor's pragmatic analysis utilizes a high ``Thinking Budget'' (8,000 tokens) to evaluate Gricean maxims across three distinct categories simultaneously, the system occasionally suffered from latency and timeout errors during peak traffic. 

When users experienced network interruptions or the Gemini API returned a \texttt{503 Service Unavailable} error, the application state was disrupted. According to the data sanitization logs generated by \texttt{sanitycheck.py}, several sessions resulted in empty transcripts or triggered a fallback mechanism that awarded a default 1.0 score across all metrics.

\begin{quote}
\textit{``My internet tweaked, so Socius gave me a 1.0 score on all categories.''} \textbf{-- P24 (Day 3)}
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
\textit{``If it can handle more traffic or demand it would be great... the demand is very high at night which causes quite late replies which is kind of frustrating.''} \textbf{-- P17 (Consolidated Feedback)}
\end{quote}

While this did not break the LLM's internal reasoning, the external infrastructure limits caused temporary frustration regarding unfair grading and disrupted the pedagogical feedback loop.

\subsection{\hl{Conversational Looping and Formulaic Phrasing (Low to Moderate Severity)}}
In scenarios lacking a rigid resolution path, the AI Partner occasionally fell into conversational loops, reiterating the same complaint despite the user's attempts to compromise. Furthermore, the prompt constraint instructing the model to use \textit{``Filipino conversational markers''} sometimes resulted in over-indexing on specific phrases.

\begin{quote}
\textit{``It kept repeating the same thing over and over again. In the `Kayo Na Ba' chat, I assured it that I wanted to be official, then it would suddenly say `seryoso ka ba' again when I thought I was making progress.''} \textbf{-- P08 (Day 2)}
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
\textit{``The chatbot became repetitive with its words at times. Each new response would always start with `hay nako' and have a similar sentence structure.''} \textbf{-- P04 (Day 4)}
\end{quote}

Because the AI Partner's thinking budget is constrained to 512 tokens to prioritize real-time typing speed over deep contextual memory, the model occasionally struggled to recognize when a user had adequately resolved the conflict, leading to these repetitive loops.

\subsection{\hl{Summary of Technical Impact}}
Despite these anomalies, the open-ended responses from the consolidated survey indicated that the bugs did not negate the system's overall pedagogical value. Participants noted that they still \textit{``felt safe to make mistakes''} and that the \textit{``realness of the scenarios''} largely compensated for the occasional robotic looping or latency. 

As one participant summarized in their final evaluation:
\begin{quote}
\textit{``Despite the error, I think it's a pretty nice and comprehensive way of being able to see where you lack in interpersonal skills, and how you can improve.''} \textbf{-- P25 (Day 1)}
\end{quote}

\section{\hl{Discussion}}
\indent This section synthesizes the findings from the various testing phases and reflects on their implications for the research objectives, system design, 
and future development directions. The discussion is organized around key themes that emerged from the data, including user engagement, cultural localization, 
psychological safety, and the limitations of the current system iteration.

\indent The primary objective of the study was to evaluate the effectiveness of Socius as a platform for practicing the navigation of complex conversational scenarios. 
The results show an interesting finding that while objectively, there was no significant change in pragmatic ability over the five-day period, users reported high levels 
of emotional investment and perceived pedagogical value. This could suggest that the chatbot's impact is rooted leess in immediate behavioral modification, and more in fostering a mindset of reflective practice, 
meta-pragmatic awareness, psychological safety, and confidence in social communication.

\subsection{Objective Change vs. Perceived Growth}
\indent A primary finding is the disconnect between objective measures of significant change in pragmatic competence and user perception. Quantitative analysis showed non-significant changes across the metrics measured, 
a result likely due to the short duration of the testing phase. As noted by \citeA{house1996}, the development of pragmatic fluency is a long-term process that requires continuous exposure and internalization.

\indent Despite this, feedback from the surveys revealed that users received "valuable feedback and advice." By providing personalized feedback, the system enhances user engagement and satisfaction. 
Users also felt more confident in their social communication skills and believed in the long-term benefits of the system. This suggests that while measurable improvements in pragmatic competence may 
require a longer time frame to manifest, users are already perceiving value in the experience. This high perceived value indicates that the system may be effectively fostering a growth mindset, and that even 
through short-term exposure, the chatbot helped users become more aware of their communication patterns and gaps.

\subsection{Authentic Conflict Simulation and the Realism of Toxic AI}
\indent The eerie realism reported by participants in high-conflict scenarios marks a notable achievement from standard pedagogical chatbots. Participants noted that scenarios felt "extremely realistic" because
of the AI Partner's ability to mimick "toxic human traits." Unlike traditional systems designed for more "polite" interactions, Socius takes advantage of Gemini's ability to navigate complex, non-cooperative dialogue.
This finding aligns with the claim that LLMs such as GPT-4 and Gemini can replicate human-like pragmatic reasoning in social interactions \cite{bojic_gpt-4_2023}. 

\indent By allowing the AI Partner to be more stubborn, defensive, or evasive, the system creates a more authentic simulation of real-life interpersonal challenges by replicating the psychological stress of 
real-world conflict. This forced users to navigate genuine face-threatening situations. The "eerieness" described suggests that the system was able to navigate the fine line between realism and user comfort, 
which is a critical design consideration for any system aiming to foster pragmatic competence. The system was able to move from a standard language exercise to an emotional simulator that demands genuine 
interpersonal resilience and adaptability. However, this also raises ethical considerations regarding the potential psychological impact on users, especially if the AI Partner's behavior becomes too 
aggressive or manipulative. Future iterations of the system may need to implement safeguards to ensure that the realism does not come at the cost of user well-being.

\subsection{AI Awareness and the Disinhibition Effect}
\indent A recurring theme in the user feedback revea;s a trade-off regarding the user's awareness of interacting with an AI. Some participants reported sacrificing genuine emotional realism because they were 
aware that they were talking to a bot. However, this awareness also led to a state of Psychological Safety and the disinhibition effect. As participant P19 noted, "because it is a robot, I can say whatever I want without repercussion."

\indent This disinhibition effect allowed users to attempt "risky" pragmatic strategies, such as direct confrontation, which they might avoid in real-life conversations due to the cultural pressure of Hiya (shame) and Pakikisama (harmonious social relations).
This aligns with the ethical and psychological considerations stated by \cite{Coghlan:2023} regarding the use of AI in social skills training, that the absence of a real human "judge" provides a "brave space" for experimentation.
Consequently, the artificiality of the partner, while reducing emotional immersion, actually enhances the pedagogical value by creating a safe environment for users to explore and practice new behaviors without fear of real-world consequences.

\subsection{Cultural Scaffolding as an Immersion Driver}
\indent The integration of Filipino cultural frameworks was cited as one of the most valued features of Socius. By embedding these concepts, the system was able to provide scenarios that felt culturally relevant and resonant, rather than generic. This
cultural scaffolding likely contributed to the system's ability to evoke authentic emotional responses, as users could easily relate the scenarios to their own lived experiences. Localizing the scenarios is important, as role-play based storytelling
is most effective when it is culturally situated \cite{Aspecto2025Kulturo}.

\indent The use of Taglish further bridged the gap between formal instruction and colloquial communication and relational practice. By grounding the AI Mentor's feedback in social logic, the system provided a level of nuance that
generic AI companions lack. This cultural relevance and specificity acted as the primary driver of engagement, which proves that in pragmatic training, the cultural context is not just a backdrop but is just as vital as the linguistic content. 

\subsection{Technical Friction and the "Naturalness" of Conversation Flow}
\indent Despite the high marks for realism, engagement, and scenario quality, the study identified friction points regarding "Chatbot Naturalness." Participants noted that the AI's responses were "too long" and "repetitive," which disrupted the flow of conversation.
This reflects a common challenge in LLM-mediated dialogues, described as the "stochastic parrot" \cite{bender_dangers_2021}, where models prioritize patterns and exhaustive output over conversational economy, brevity, and relevance.

\indent Furthermore, the "opaque and unclear" feedback reported by users suggests that the AI Mentor's analysis may not be effectively communicating its insights in a way that is easily digestible. When users are given a score without an intuitive breakdown of what led to that score,
it leads to frustration and a sense of disconnect between the feedback and the user's own perception of their performance. This echoes a critique of LLM-based applications, which emphasize the need for explainability and transparency in AI-generated feedback \cite{Zhou:2024}.
Additionally, the AI Mentor being perceived as too text-heavy and passive suggests that future iterations could benefit from more interactive and dialogic feedback, rather than descriptive.

\indent In summary, Socius showcases that the strength of an AI learning tool lies in its capacity for contextualized conflict. By moving away from polite AI, and embracing the eerie realism of toxic
human behaviors within a Filipino cultural framework, the system creates a unique space for users to practice navigating complex social dynamics in a high-stakes yet safe environment. While immediate behavioral shifts may require more than five days to manifest, the system's contribution is the development 
of a "reflective mirror" where users analyze and reflect on their own communicative patterns and strategies. Future development must focus on refining the naturalness of the conversation flow and enhancing the clarity and actionability of the AI Mentor's feedback to maximize the system's pedagogical impact 
and bridge the remaining gap between simulation and natural human conversation.
