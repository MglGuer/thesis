\chapter{1. Introduction}\label{sec:intro}


\section{Background of the Study}

\indent \hl{Pragmatics is a subfield of linguistics that examines the contribution of context to the meaning of a message.
Unlike grammar or semantics, which focus on structural correctness and literal interpretation,
pragmatics deals with the intention, implication, and appropriateness of a statement during social interactions. It involves
going beyond the surface level, looking for the implied meaning rather than the literal meaning. It is essentially how speakers navigate relevance,
politeness, and indirectness depending on social context \cite{marasabessy_pragmatic_2023}. Individuals experiencing difficulties with pragmatics 
are more susceptible to instances of miscommunication or social isolation.}

\hl{Many individuals struggle with real-world social interactions, yet there is a lack of} accessible, judgment-free spaces to practice and refine 
\hl{their communication skills}. Despite recent advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Large Language Models (LLMs), their potential for 
supporting social communication training remains underexplored. Most existing chatbots are not designed to promote adaptive and context-sensitive 
conversation practice, \hl{which leaves} a critical gap in available tools. A chatbot focused on enhancing pragmatic competence designed for the general
population could provide a low-risk environment to build confidence and conversational fluency in individuals.

The need for pragmatics training is not limited to neurodivergent individuals. Pragmatics plays a vital role in grounding meaning through 
context \cite{fried_pragmatics_2023}, which is vital for effective communication and conversation. This highlights the broader application of pragmatics 
training as a way to improve real-world communicative competence across diverse settings. Furthermore,\cite{gibbs_pragmatics_2020} argue that 
pragmatic performance is influenced by cultural expectations, personal histories, and situational variability. These are factors that affect all individuals,
regardless of diagnostic status. Their work suggests that understanding and practicing pragmatic nuance can lead to more meaningful and effective 
social engagement across the board, thereby maximizing its potential benefit and broad applicability.

Recent work in social skill training has introduced the AI Partner and AI Mentor (APAM) framework as a promising model
for combining simulated practice and pedagogical feedback using Large Language Models (LLMs) \cite{yang_social_2024}.
In this framework, the AI Partner engages the user in realistic, domain-specific conversational scenarios, serving as 
a practice partner that reflects varied social contexts. The AI Mentor, on the other hand, observes the interaction and 
provides theory-grounded feedback, contextual suggestions, and reflective insights to promote social learning. This
dual-agent structure is particularly effective for training pragmatic competence, as it allows users to explore 
conversational nuance while receiving guidance grounded in expert frameworks like Grice’s Maxims. The APAM framework 
supports scalable, low-risk social communication training and has been applied to domains such as counseling, education,
and conflict resolution. In this study, APAM is adapted to focus specifically on one-on-one, pragmatic communication, 
with the AI Partner simulating conversations that test the user’s ability to navigate implicature and context, and the
AI Mentor offering reflective feedback based on violations or observance of conversational maxims.

Grice's Cooperative Principle is a key theoretical framework for pragmatic analysis. It includes four maxims: Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner. 
These maxims guide how speakers are expected to contribute to conversations, which is by being informative, truthful, relevant, and clear \cite{hossain_application_2021}. 
When these maxims are followed, communication tends to proceed smoothly. \hl{Should violations occur}, intentional or not, these may lead to confusion, implicature, or breakdowns. 
\st{Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4 have been evaluated on Gricean tasks and, in some cases, outperform humans in identifying implicature or recovering unstated meaning} \mbox{\cite{bojic_gpt-4_2023}}\st{. Datasets such as GRICE have even been created to assess AI's ability to handle nuanced conversational reasoning} \mbox{\cite{zheng_grice_2021}}\st{.}

\hl{Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4 and Gemini 3 have been evaluated on Gricean tasks and, in some cases, they outperform humans in identifying implicature or 
recovering unstated meaning} \mbox{\cite{bojic_gpt-4_2023}}\hl{. However, achieving true pragmatic competence in an educational tool requires more than recognizing 
logical maxims. It also demands an understanding of developmental psychology and cultural context. For young adults navigating the transition to adulthood, 
conversational challenges are deeply tied to identity exploration and changing social expectations (Arnett, 2000; Havighurst, 1972). 
Furthermore, in the Philippine context, communication is heavily shaped by cultural values such as 
\textit{Pakikisama} (smooth interpersonal relations), \textit{Hiya} (sense of shame or face), \textit{Utang na Loob} (debt of gratitude), and \textit{Paggalang} (respect) (Jocano, 1997).}

In practical chatbot interactions, these maxims are regularly tested. For example, an LLM might provide overly verbose
answers (violating Quantity), respond off-topic (Relation), or misinterpret ambiguous intent (Manner). These outcomes
are not necessarily “bad” but \hl{are} rather reflective of the model’s training data, lack of world knowledge, or inability
to interpret non-verbal cues. A study comparing various LLM generations noted a steady improvement in pragmatic handling,
suggesting that training scale and model design significantly affect performance \cite{bojic_gpt-4_2023}. Yet, challenges still remain,
such as repeated pragmatic failures leading users to simplify their speech over time, which may erode nuanced communication habits
\cite{eragamreddy_impact_2025}.

Despite advances in natural language processing and the increasing presence of conversational agents in daily life,
most AI systems still struggle with pragmatic competence, or the ability to interpret and generate language that
is appropriate to a social context. While these systems can produce grammatically correct and coherent responses, they
often fail to grasp implied meanings, respond appropriately to indirect speech acts, or adapt to the relational dynamics
of a conversation. This limitation becomes more noticeable in one-on-one interactions where subtle social cues and contextual
relevance are critical. This raises important questions: Can a chatbot be designed to recognize and respond to pragmatic
violations? How can feedback be delivered in a way that helps users reflect on and improve their communication strategies?
These questions highlight an opportunity to bridge the gap between linguistic fluency and social competence
by developing a chatbot that supports pragmatic learning through context-sensitive interactions and theory-informed
feedback.

While current chatbot applications focus more on therapeutic services, such as Woebot \cite{Woebot:2025}, there is a lack of tools designed for non-therapeutic 
needs, such as for enhancing social communication skills. Moreover, many young adults (\hl{ages} 15–24) struggle with real-world social interactions but lack accessible, 
judgment-free spaces to practice. Existing chatbots are not designed for adaptive social communication training, often failing to accommodate diverse 
communication styles or provide meaningful, real-time feedback.

\section{Research Objectives}

\indent This study aims to develop a one-on-one chatbot that helps young adults ages 15 to 24 years old improve their social communication skills. 
Unlike task-based chatbots, it will simulate natural conversations, provide real-time feedback, and offer a safe space for practicing social interactions. 
The objectives of the study are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \st{To develop a chatbot grounded in the APAM framework that simulates natural one-on-one conversations as an AI Partner, enabling users to interact in contexts requiring pragmatic awareness.} \hl{To design and implement a dual-agent architecture (AI Partner and AI Mentor) utilizing the Google Gemini API to facilitate a natural roleplay experience and deep pragmatic analysis.}
    \item \st{To design realistic contexts and situations that young adults are likely to encounter in academic, professional, and social environments, providing them with opportunities to practice and refine their pragmatic skills.} \hl{To formulate a theoretical framework for scenario generation that synthesizes Grice's Cooperative Principle, Developmental Psychology (Havighurst and Arnett), and Filipino Cultural Values.}
    \item \hl{To develop a multi-level, quantitative scoring rubric for pragmatic competence—enforced via JSON Schema Controlled Generation—that provides detailed, actionable metrics across Intention Coherence, Social \& Affective Nuance, and Communicative Effectiveness.}
    \item To evaluate the chatbot's effectiveness in enhancing users' pragmatic competence and social communication skills through user feedback \hl{and human user testing}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Limitations}
\indent \st{The chatbot will be developed using the APAM framework, emphasizing situation emulation and the provision of feedback. The primary scope of this feedback is its direct alignment with Grice's Maxims (Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner) and the recognition of implicatures, serving as the explicit framework for assessing and guiding user improvement.} \hl{The chatbot was developed using the AI Partner - AI Mentor (APAM) framework, emphasizing situation emulation and the provision of personalized feedback. The primary scope of this feedback encompasses a multi-level scoring framework assessing three core domains: Intention Coherence (logical flow and speech acts), Social \& Affective Nuance (formality, empathy, and informativeness), and Communicative Effectiveness (goal achievement and implicature handling). While grounded in Grice's Maxims (Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner), the system expands upon them to include developmental psychology and cultural pragmatics.} Moreover, these maxims and implicatures are not reliant on paralinguistic cues, making them suitable for implementation in a text-based AI system \cite{dale_role_1996}. 

\st{In line with goal-oriented language generation, the chatbot's responses are 
designed to reflect Gricean principles without the need to explicitly enforce them as rigid rules. Its capacity to deliver pragmatic guidance will rely on machine learning 
models, providing feedback based on pre-defined pragmatic rules rather than on human-like comprehension or nuanced, context-specific human judgment. 
Accordingly, the effectiveness of the system is dependent on the alignment between Gemini's pragmatic ability and the Gricean maxims, and the feedback's effectiveness is 
contingent on the chatbot's accurate identification of pragmatic violations or successes, a task made challenging by the inherent ambiguity of human communication and the 
limitations of current natural language processing (NLP). A potential gap is that while the feedback targets Grice's Maxims and implicatures, it may not comprehensively cover 
all facets of pragmatic competence, such as politeness strategies or speech act theory, which are outside the immediate scope of measurement for this particular intervention.}

\hl{In line with goal-oriented language generation, the chatbot relies on the reasoning ability of the Gemini 3 model family rather than a rigidly fine-tuned local model. 
Feedback is strictly enforced through a JSON Output Schema via the Gemini API, ensuring machine-parsable, quantitative metrics for longitudinal tracking without sacrificing 
natural language understanding. A potential limitation is that the system relies on the LLM's probabilistic evaluation of pragmatic violations rather than nuanced, 
context-specific human judgment. Furthermore, while the system effectively captures Gricean maxims and basic Filipino cultural values 
(e.g., \textit{Pakikisama}, \textit{Hiya}, \textit{Paggalang}), it may not comprehensively cover all facets of non-verbal or highly localized sociolinguistic phenomena.}

\st{The study will define conversational scenarios that reflect academic, professional, and casual social contexts relevant to young adults. This is best practiced since these are general situations that young adults would encounter everyday, most especially that they would build connections or relationships within these environments. These scenarios will serve as structured environments for practicing pragmatic competence. These situations will be constructed based the situations present in the GRICE dataset, wherein Grice's maxims and implicatures are implemented. However, in order to ensure relevance to the local context as Grice's theory of conversation is primarily a western theory, rules will be given to Gemini in order to include culturally specific references and language usage to better reflect local Filipino cultural norms and social dynamics.} 

\hl{The study defined four core conversational themes reflecting academic, professional, and casual social contexts relevant to young adults: Identity vs. Expectation, 
Autonomy vs. Dependence, Ambiguity vs. Definition, and Group vs. Self. To ensure relevance to the local context, explicit ``negative constraints" and conversational 
marker rules were injected into the LLM's system instructions. This forced the AI to utilize appropriate Taglish (a mix of Tagalog and English) and culturally 
specific emotional code-switching without breaking character or providing unnatural English translations.} 

Careful review and adaptation \st{will be} \hl{were} carried out to preserve the original pragmatic intent while making the content accessible and 
relatable to Filipino users. The focus of the chatbot is on verbal communication, thereby the conversations presented in a text-based format would reflect real-world 
conversations. The target participants will also strictly involve young adults aged 15 - 24. These participants need not be considered neurodivergent. 
This decision is based on the principle that pragmatic competence is a universal and developmental skill set, not exclusively a challenge for individuals with diagnosed 
conditions. The study’s goal is to create a broadly applicable educational tool for enhancing social communication, rather than a clinical intervention for a specific population.

The evaluation of the chatbot will be conducted through user interaction data and self-reported feedback. The assessment will focus on the user's perception of the chatbot's 
ability to simulate contextually appropriate conversation whilst providing pragmatically-aligned feedback after the conversation. 
Thus, this is based on the user's self-reported experience on the conversation authenticity. It is important to note that limitations may arise due to 
cultural differences between the \st{Gemini's training data} \hl{base LLM's training data} and the target population. Some participants may not be able to fully relate to 
or understand certain \st{scenarios provided} \hl{thematic conflicts}, especially if the scenario is not appropriate for their age group. These potential mismatches 
may affect user engagement or interpretation and will be considered in the analysis of user feedback to inform future improvements.

% The study will limit its target demographic to those who are between 15 to 24 years old, aligning with the World Health Organization's (WHO, n.d.) definition of youth. This age range was chosen as young adults often face crucial transitions in academic, social, and professional settings where effective communication plays a key role. The chatbot will also be limited to text-based input and will not incorporate voice or video-based input. It will exclusively use the English language due to the availability of numerous datasets suitable for training. The study also aims to identify the different strategies used by experts in teaching communication skills to young adults, especially those who are deemed socially awkward. Furthermore, the chatbot itself will provide the situations and contexts that the user will experience, rather than having the context supplied by the user. In doing so, the study defines assistance strategies related to helping young adults with social awkwardness and pragmatics training that can be implemented in a chatbot. Moreover, the research will focus on the user's actual responses, particularly their word choices, rather than how they audibly deliver those responses. The study will also concentrate on the subtopic of social communication—specifically, pragmatics—which will be measured and assessed using Grice's Maxims and the concept of implicature.

\section{Significance of the Study}

\indent This study offers a theory-informed chatbot that incorporates pragmatic and conversational linguistics, particularly Grice's Maxims and the APAM framework, 
into an interactive training tool for students to develop their social communication skills. 
The key output is a dual-role system: an AI Partner that engages in one-on-one conversations grounded in real-world scenarios, and an AI Mentor that evaluates the user's 
pragmatic performance and offers reflective feedback based on observed violations or adherence to conversational norms. As such, this study \hl{also contributes} to the 
understanding and exploration of an AI-driven approach to teaching, in general, a sociolinguistic skill for young adults. 

\st{Furthermore, In contrast to prior chatbot implementations using LLMs other than Gemini, this study explores the use of Gemini as an alternative large language model. The goal is not to evaluate its superiority, but to understand its potential to model pragmatic competence in an educational chatbot context.} 

\hl{Furthermore, a significant contribution of this research lies in its architectural findings regarding LLM deployment for pedagogical tasks. 
The study demonstrates that for complex, theory-driven communication coaching, the advanced reasoning and in-context learning capabilities of a prompt-engineered 
generalist model (Gemini 3 Flash) significantly outperforms smaller models fine-tuned on narrow, specialized datasets. Additionally, the introduction of a 
``Holistic Mentor" feature, which aggregates longitudinal data across multiple sessions via a Supabase backend, provides a novel approach to tracking and coaching 
user progression over time, rather than limiting support to isolated per-session feedback.}   

Another significance of this research is its potential to benefit individuals within the specified age range. Being able to engage in conversation is a vital skill as it 
affects \hl{an individual's} personal relationships, casual and professional interactions, and self-confidence. Moreover, the results from this study will be pertinent to educators, 
counselors, and researchers in the field of linguistics as it provides insights into how AI can be used in an educational manner as a way of providing another avenue of 
learning for young adults. The chatbot would be able to provide a safe environment for young adults to practice their conversational ability without the need of a human 
partner, thereby being interactive, judgement-free, and low-risk. This may also be beneficial for those who struggle with face-to-face interactions, such as those with social anxiety or the 
socially awkward. By integrating social theories such as Grice’s Maxims and the APAM framework—into the chatbot’s design, this research contributes a structured, theory-based 
approach to teaching pragmatic competence. Specifically, the conversation flows developed in this study may serve as practical templates for future AI-driven tools aimed at 
improving real-world communication skills in educational and therapeutic contexts.

By equipping young individuals with the ability to communicate more effectively, this research holds broader implications for society at large. As pragmatic competence is 
crucial not only for personal growth but also for constructive social participation, enhancing this skill among \hl{the} youth can lead to more socially-aware communities. 
In the Philippine context, where communication is often shaped by high-context norms, indirectness, and respect for social hierarchies, \hl{being rooted in} cultural values such as 
Pakikisama, Hiya, and Utang na Loob, the ability to navigate pragmatic cues is especially vital (Jocano, 1997; Nadar, 1999; Miranda, 1998). By modeling these dynamics through 
the chatbot, this study helps Filipino youth engage more effectively in culturally appropriate interactions across educational and social domains.

\indent To achieve the objectives of this study, the methodology involves the design, development, and evaluation of a pragmatically-aware chatbot system grounded in the APAM 
(AI Partner–AI Mentor) framework. The methodology is structured into seven major phases: 
(1) Knowledge Base Construction, (2) Backend Development, (3) Frontend Development, (4) Expert Validation of Scenarios, 
(5) User Validation and Testing Period, (6) Data Analysis, and (7) Documentation and Report Writing.
The details of these activities are as follows:

\subsection{\st{Knowledge Base Construction} \hl{Theoretical Framework Formulation and Scenario Generation}}
\indent \st{The chatbot's pragmatics training capability lies in a properly curated and theory-driven knowledge base. The GRICE dataset, which consists of dialogue examples structured around Grice's Maxims, will be the foundation with which the chatbot will be trained on. Each dialogue is designed to highlight either adherence to or violation of these maxims, providing concrete examples of pragmatic competence. The scenarios constructed by the LLM will be validated by an expert before being stored in the scenario database for use by the chatbot.

To supplement the dataset, additional conversational prompts were generated using Gemini with explicit prompts to align with the same pragmatic structures. The dataset is found in a public GitHub repository, making it open source while additional dialogues or scenarios will be synthetically generated by AI. This ensures that there are no copyright or licensing concerns.

The GRICE dataset (Zheng et al., 2021), used in this research, is publicly available and was released as part of the Findings of ACL-IJCNLP 2021. It is © 2021 Association for Computational Linguistics. The dataset is used here strictly for academic and non-commercial research purposes, in accordance with ACL's standard terms of use. No modifications or redistribution of the dataset are performed in this study.}

\hl{Rather than relying exclusively on an existing static dataset, the chatbot's pragmatics training capability was built upon a multi-pillar theoretical framework. 
Scenarios were dynamically structured around four recurring developmental themes synthesized from Arnett's Theory of Emerging Adulthood and Havighurst's Developmental Tasks: 
(1) Identity vs. Expectation, (2) Autonomy vs. Dependence, (3) Ambiguity vs. Definition, and (4) Group vs. Self. Each scenario was explicitly coded with a defined conflict, 
a conversational formula, and a specific user goal. To ensure cultural resonance, the scenarios and AI instructions were injected with constraints mapping to 
Filipino cultural values (\textit{Pakikisama}, \textit{Hiya}, \textit{Utang na Loob}) (Jocano, 1997).}

\subsection{Software Development}
\indent \hl{The chatbot, named Socius, is implemented using the Python programming language and integrates the Gemini LLM via its API. 
This exploratory use of Gemini aims to examine how its conversational capabilities align with the APAM framework and Gricean principles, without making prior assumptions about 
its effectiveness. The chatbot follows a six-stage conversational loop: (1) Session Initialization, (2) Scenario Selection and Instantiation, (3) Conversation Simulation, \
(4) Pragmatic Logging, (5) Conversation Analysis, and (6) Metric Generation.}

\hl{Each stage is designed to assess the user’s pragmatic competence, specifically their adherence to Grice’s Maxims within various social scenarios generated and seeded through 
the system. During the conversation, the chatbot assumes the role of an AI Partner, adopting a context-specific persona to engage the user in conversation. After the exchange, it shifts 
to its AI Mentor role, where it analyzes the transcript, logs maxim violations, and evaluates the user’s responses based on relevance, informativeness, clarity, and recognition of 
implied meaning. The system then generates feedback and performance metrics, enabling reflection and, optionally, scenario retry to support iterative learning.

The stages of software development are as follows:}

\subsubsection{Backend Development}
\indent \st{Backend development will focus on the core conversational engine built using Python, integrating the Gemini API to handle response generation. This will involve developing the system's main components as outlined in the architectural design. Particularly, the backend will handle session management, scenario selection, and database creation (including user and conversation logging). Moreover, the development will include the implementation of the pragmatic analyzer, the feedback generation, the conversation flow manager, and the calculation of implicature accuracy metrics.}

\hl{Backend development focused on the core conversational engine built using Python and Flask, integrating the \texttt{google-genai} SDK to handle response generation. 
The backend managed session isolation, scenario selection, and database operations using Supabase. Crucially, the development included the implementation of the 
Pragmatic Analyzer via JSON Schema Controlled Generation, ensuring feedback was structured into actionable numerical scores and text summaries rather than relying on 
generic text generation.}

\subsubsection{Frontend Development}
\indent \st{Frontend development will focus on} \hl{Frontend development focused on} creating the user interface (UI). The UI \st{will be} \hl{was} designed as a 
text-messaging application to provide a familiar and intuitive user experience for text-based interaction and feedback delivery. Key features \st{will} include a 
chat window for real-time conversation with the chatbot and a component for selecting from predefined social scenarios. The interface \st{will present} \hl{presents} 
alternating message colors and timestamps to clearly distinguish between user and chatbot messages. The development \st{will also include creating} \hl{also includes} 
a user profile section where users can track their progress \st{based on implicature accuracy metrics} \hl{through the Holistic Mentor dashboard} across multiple sessions. 
The design \st{will prioritize} \hl{prioritizes} usability and accessibility through simple graphical elements.

\subsection{Expert Validation and Architectural Pivot}
\indent The conversational scenarios generated for the chatbot \st{will undergo} \hl{underwent} expert validation. 
These scenarios \st{are created through the Gemini LLM} \hl{were generated using Gemini 3.0 Pro} and were designed by the researchers to reflect the theoretical 
framework of Grice's Maxims and Implicatures. Thus, it is important to ensure that these scenarios are relevant, culturally appropriate, and aligned with the intended learning 
outcomes. To accomplish this, an expert in \hl{the philosophy of language}\st{, behavioral science, or any related field} was consulted to review the scenarios that have been constructed based on the 
GRICE dataset \cite{zheng_grice_2021}. This validation process will ensure that the scenarios are not only grounded in pragmatic theory but are also appropriate and relevant 
for the target demographic of young adults aged 15-24. The expert \st{will assess} \hl{assessed} whether the scenarios effectively \st{emulate} \hl{emulated} 
real-world academic, professional, and social contexts and whether the localized examples for the Filipino context maintain their intended pragmatic meaning. 
Only scenarios approved by the expert \st{will be} \hl{were} added to the scenario database for use in the chatbot interactions.

\hl{Initial architectural experiments involved an attempt to fine-tune a smaller open-source model (Mistral-7B) on the GRICE dataset \mbox{\cite{zheng_grice_2021}} 
to serve as the AI Mentor. However, functional testing revealed that this approach yielded brittle, overly literal feedback that failed to capture cultural nuances or 
provide supportive pedagogical advice. Consequently, the system was successfully pivoted to a prompt-engineered architecture utilizing the superior reasoning capabilities 
of Gemini 3. The newly generated conversational scenarios and the multi-level scoring rubric underwent expert review to ensure they accurately reflected real-world academic, 
professional, and social contexts relevant to Filipino young adults aged 15-24, maintaining their intended pragmatic relevance without relying on the original rigid dataset.}

\subsection{Validation and Testing Period}
\subsubsection{User Validation}
\indent User testing will be conducted with volunteer participants, consequently needing valid participants. Before engaging with the study, 
participants will enter a two-part process. The initial stage is a need-finding study to confirm the need for a pragmatic skills training tool, and to verify the target audience's interest and willingness to use the chatbot. This involves distributing a survey (Google Form), namely the Pre-Assessment Form.

\subsubsection{Participant Selection}
\indent The participant selection process will begin with recruiting volunteers from the target demographic of young adults aged 15 to 24. 
A convenience sampling method will be employed, utilizing online announcements and social media channels to distribute the Pre-Assessment Form to a broad audience.
The primary inclusion criteria for the study will be participants falling within the specified age range and providing informed consent via the form. 

From the pool of eligible respondents, a purposive sampling strategy will then be used to select a target sample of approximately 20 participants for the chatbot interaction 
phase. This purposive approach will aim to create a diverse cohort, representing a spectrum of self-reported communication confidence levels and varying degrees of interest in 
using a chatbot solution, as identified from their Pre-Assessment Form answers. This method ensures that the final participant group is not skewed toward a single profile but 
is instead representative of the broad audience for which Socius is designed.

\subsubsection{Pre-Assessment Form}
\indent \hl{The Pre-Assessment Form will gather demographic information such as age, name, and contact details, as well as the participant's experience with social 
communication and chatbots. It will then involve the following sections: (1) Introduction and Consent, (2) Participant's General Communication Confidence, 
(3) Participant's Pragmatic Skill Self-Assessment, (4) Attitudes towards a Chatbot-based Solution. 

This will focus on gathering the participant's self-reported data on their communication habits, challenges, and their openness to a technological solution. 
The Introduction and Consent section will first clarify the study's objective to understand communication experiences and ensure the participant agrees to proceed 
by detailing all the necessary details, stated in the informed consent form, about the study the participant must understand prior to consenting to the study. 
Furthermore, minors must fill out a Parent's Consent form to proceed with the Pre-Assessment form. This will also reassure the participant that they may withdraw 
at any time within the study, and that their data will be anonymized and used solely for research purposes. The data will be immediately destroyed once the study is completed.

The second section, Participant's General Communication Confidence, will employ a Likert scale to establish a baseline of how comfortable participants feel in various common social contexts, such as speaking in groups or meeting new people. The Participant's Pragmatic Skill Self-Assessment section will then explore 
specific conversational behaviors related to pragmatic competence. This includes self-perceived ability in areas such as providing an appropriate amount of information, staying on topic, and interpreting non-literal meanings like sarcasm or indirect hints.

Finally, the section on Attitudes towards a Chatbot-based Solution will directly assess the need for and interest in the Socius chatbot. This part will gauge how likely the participant would be to use such a tool, what their primary concerns might be (e.g., privacy, effectiveness), and what features they would find most valuable.
}

\paragraph{Orientation Session}
\indent \hl{Once the final cohort of approximately 20 participants is selected, each individual will undergo a brief orientation. This orientation may be delivered via a standardized instruction sheet and email. 
The purpose is to ensure all participants begin the study with a clear and consistent understanding of their tasks. The orientation materials will:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \hl{Briefly reiterate the purpose of the study.}
\item \hl{Provide clear instructions on how to access and log in to the Socius chatbot.}
\item \hl{Outline the expected level of participation (e.g., "Please engage with at least three conversational scenarios over the course of one week").}
\item \hl{Inform them that their (anonymized) conversation data will be logged for analysis and that they will be asked to complete a final survey after the interaction period.}
\item \hl{Provide contact information for the researchers in case they encounter technical difficulties or have questions.}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Chatbot Interaction Period}
\indent\hl{Following the orientation, the main experimental phase will begin, lasting for a predefined period of three weeks. During this time, participants are free to 
interact with the Socius chatbot at their own convenience. Their primary task is to engage in conversations within the scenarios provided by the chatbot,
practicing their pragmatic communication skills in a simulated environment. All interactions, including the participant's inputs and the chatbot's responses, 
will be automatically and anonymously logged in the backend database for subsequent analysis.}

\subsection{Data Analysis}
\indent Quantitative testing will be done to assess the usability and effectiveness of Socius. This may include questions such as user experience, satisfaction, and chatbot authenticity, usefulness, and effectiveness.
Additionally, the user will give their perceived improvement in their pragmatic competence as another factor of the chatbot's effectiveness.


\subsubsection{Chatbot Performance Evaluation}
\indent \hl{The chatbot's performance will be evaluated through expert analysis of its conversational abilities. The same expert who validated the scenarios will 
analyze chat logs from interactions to assess whether the chatbot's responses are pragmatically aligned with Grice's Maxims. This evaluation will focus on the 
chatbot's ability to maintain a consistent persona, generate contextually appropriate replies, and accurately handle implicatures, thus also utilizing the 
implicature accuracies to metricize itself, as both an AI Partner and an AI Mentor. The feedback from this evaluation will be used to refine the chatbot's 
conversational logic and ensure its effectiveness as a training tool before it is deployed for user testing.
In addition to these metrics, the weekly survey will include several open-ended questions to gather qualitative insights. Participants will be asked to describe 
the most and least helpful aspects of their experience, and to reflect on whether they have attempted to apply any learned strategies in their real-life conversations.}
