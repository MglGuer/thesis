\chapter{1. Introduction}\label{sec:intro}


\section{Background of the Study}

Pragmatics, a subfield of linguistics, discusses how context contributes to meaning in communication.
Pragmatics, unlike grammar or semantics, which focus on structural correctness and literal interpretation, 
is more involved with intention, implication, and appropriateness within social interactions. It involves 
understanding what is meant beyond what is said; meaning over literal. In essence, it is how speakers navigate relevance,
politeness, and indirectness depending on social context \cite{marasabessy_pragmatic_2023}. For individuals with social communication
challenges, such as those with neurodivergence or social anxiety, difficulties in pragmatic interpretation may result in 
social isolation or miscommunication.

Recent work in social skill training has introduced the APAM (AI Partner and AI Mentor) framework as a promising model
for combining simulated practice and pedagogical feedback using large language models (LLMs) \cite{yang_social_2024}.
In this framework, the AI Partner engages the user in realistic, domain-specific conversational scenarios, serving as 
a practice partner that reflects varied social contexts. The AI Mentor, on the other hand, observes the interaction and 
provides theory-grounded feedback, contextual suggestions, and reflective insights to promote social learning. This
dual-agent structure is particularly effective for training pragmatic competence, as it allows users to explore 
conversational nuance while receiving guidance grounded in expert frameworks like Grice’s Maxims. The APAM framework 
supports scalable, low-risk social communication training and has been applied to domains such as counseling, education,
and conflict resolution. In this study, APAM is adapted to focus specifically on one-on-one, pragmatic communication, 
with the AI Partner simulating conversations that test the user’s ability to navigate implicature and context, and the
AI Mentor offering reflective feedback based on violations or observance of conversational maxims.

Grice's Cooperative Priniciple is a key theoretical framework for pragmatic analysis. It includes four maxims: Quantity,
Quality, Relation, and Manner. These maxims guide how pseakers are expected to contribute to conversations, which is by 
being informative, truthful, relevant, and clear \cite{hossain_application_2021}. When these maxims are followed, communication
tends to proceed smmothly. However, vioaltions, intentional or not, may lead to confusion, implicature, or breakdowns.
Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4 have been evaluated on Gricean tasks and, in some cases, outperform
humans in identifying implicature or recovering unstated meaning \cite{bojic_gpt-4_2023}. Datasets such as GRICE have even
been created to assess AI's ability to handle nuanced conversational reasoning \cite{zheng_grice_2021}.

In practical chatbot interactions, these maxims are regularly tested. For example, an LLM might provide overly verbose
answers (violating Quantity), respond off-topic (Relation), or misinterpret ambiguous intent (Manner). These outcomes
are not necessarily “bad” but rather reflective of the model’s training data, lack of world knowledge, or inability
to interpret non-verbal cues. A study comparing various LLM generations noted a steady improvement in pragmatic handling,
suggesting that training scale and model design significantly affect performance \cite{bojic_gpt-4_2023}. Yet, challenges still remain,
such as repeated pragmatic failures lead users to simplify their speech over time, which may erode nuanced communication habits
\cite{eragamreddy_impact_2025}.

Given this, this study proposes a pragmatically-aware, one-on-one chatbot designed to support social communication
practice. Rather than focusing on transactional or task-oriented dialogue, the system is intended to simulate natural,
open-ended conversations where users can practice responses, receive feedback, and experience varied situational prompts
based on Gricean analysis. Performance will not be judged as successful or not in absolute terms; instead, it will be
examined through the lens of pragmatic coherence — whether the chatbot’s output aligns with contextual expectations,
and why certain (mis)interpretations occur.

\section{Research Objectives}

This study aims to develop a one-on-one chatbot that helps young adults ages 15 to 24 years old improve their social communication skills. Unlike task-based chatbots, it will simulate natural conversations, provide real-time feedback, and offer a safe space for practicing social interactions. The objectives of the study are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item To design and develop a chatbot grounded in the APAM framework that simulates natural  one-on-one conversations as an AI Partner, enabling users to interact in contexts requiring pragmatic awareness.
    \item To integrate reflective feedback mechanisms that help users identify and improve their use of pragmatic strategies, including adherence to Grice’s Maxims and recognition of implicatures.
    \item To develop realistic contexts and situations that young adults are likely to encounter in academic, professional, and social environments, providing them with opportunities to practice and refine their pragmatic skills.
    \item To evaluate the chatbot’s effectiveness in enhancing users' pragmatic competence and social communication skills through user feedback and performance metrics.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Limitations}

The study will limit its target demographic to those who are between 15 to 24 years old, aligning with the World Health Organization's (WHO, n.d.) definition of youth. This age range was chosen as young adults often face crucial transitions in academic, social, and professional settings where effective communication plays a key role. The chatbot will also be limited to text-based input and will not incorporate voice or video-based input. It will exclusively use the English language due to the availability of numerous datasets suitable for training. The study also aims to identify the different strategies used by experts in teaching communication skills to young adults, especially those who are deemed socially awkward. Furthermore, the chatbot itself will provide the situations and contexts that the user will experience, rather than having the context supplied by the user. In doing so, the study defines assistance strategies related to helping young adults with social awkwardness and pragmatics training that can be implemented in a chatbot. Moreover, the research will focus on the user's actual responses, particularly their word choices, rather than how they audibly deliver those responses. The study will also concentrate on the subtopic of social communication—specifically, pragmatics—which will be measured and assessed using Grice's Maxims and the concept of implicature.

\section{Significance of the Study}
% Placeholder text
While current chatbot applications focus more on therapeutic services, such as Woebot \cite{Woebot:2025}, there is a lack of tools designed for non-therapeutic needs, such as for enhancing social communication skills. Moreover, Many young adults (15–24) struggle with real-world social interactions but lack accessible, judgment-free spaces to practice. Existing chatbots are not designed for adaptive social communication training, often failing to accommodate diverse communication styles or provide meaningful, real-time feedback. Thus, this study aims to provide a personalized, accessible platform for young adults who wish to improve their conversation skills.

Another significance of this research is its potential to benefit individuals within a specific age range. Being able to engage in conversation is a vital skill as it affects one’s personal relationships, casual and professional interactions, and self-confidence. By making use of a chatbot that simulates one-on-one conversations, users will be able to practice their communication skills in a low-pressure environment. This is particularly beneficial for those who struggle with face-to-face interactions, such as those with social anxiety or the socially awkward. 

Additionally, this study contributes to the reduction of bias in large language models (LLMs). Further review on the bias that affects the way LLMs interpret and respond to different communication styles, particularly those of neurodivergent individuals, can help improve their inclusivity and accuracy. By analyzing how LLMs process and adapt to diverse social behaviors, this study aims to refine chatbot interactions to be more accommodating to both neurotypical and neurodivergent users. This contributes to broader AI fairness efforts by ensuring that conversational AI tools do not reinforce stereotypes or exclude users with different communication preferences.
