\chapter{1. Introduction}\label{sec:intro}


\section{Background of the Study}

Pragmatics, a subfield of linguistics, discusses how context contributes to meaning in communication.
Pragmatics, unlike grammar or semantics, which focus on structural correctness and literal interpretation, 
is more involved with intention, implication, and appropriateness within social interactions. It involves 
understanding what is meant beyond what is said; meaning over literal. In essence, it is how speakers navigate relevance,
politeness, and indirectness depending on social context \cite{marasabessy_pragmatic_2023}. For individuals with social communication
challenges, such as those with neurodivergence or social anxiety, difficulties in pragmatic interpretation may result in 
social isolation or miscommunication.

Recent work in social skill training has introduced the APAM (AI Partner and AI Mentor) framework as a promising model
for combining simulated practice and pedagogical feedback using large language models (LLMs) \cite{yang_social_2024}.
In this framework, the AI Partner engages the user in realistic, domain-specific conversational scenarios, serving as 
a practice partner that reflects varied social contexts. The AI Mentor, on the other hand, observes the interaction and 
provides theory-grounded feedback, contextual suggestions, and reflective insights to promote social learning. This
dual-agent structure is particularly effective for training pragmatic competence, as it allows users to explore 
conversational nuance while receiving guidance grounded in expert frameworks like Grice’s Maxims. The APAM framework 
supports scalable, low-risk social communication training and has been applied to domains such as counseling, education,
and conflict resolution. In this study, APAM is adapted to focus specifically on one-on-one, pragmatic communication, 
with the AI Partner simulating conversations that test the user’s ability to navigate implicature and context, and the
AI Mentor offering reflective feedback based on violations or observance of conversational maxims.

Grice's Cooperative Priniciple is a key theoretical framework for pragmatic analysis. It includes four maxims: Quantity,
Quality, Relation, and Manner. These maxims guide how pseakers are expected to contribute to conversations, which is by 
being informative, truthful, relevant, and clear \cite{hossain_application_2021}. When these maxims are followed, communication
tends to proceed smmothly. However, vioaltions, intentional or not, may lead to confusion, implicature, or breakdowns.
Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4 have been evaluated on Gricean tasks and, in some cases, outperform
humans in identifying implicature or recovering unstated meaning \cite{bojic_gpt-4_2023}. Datasets such as GRICE have even
been created to assess AI's ability to handle nuanced conversational reasoning \cite{zheng_grice_2021}.

In practical chatbot interactions, these maxims are regularly tested. For example, an LLM might provide overly verbose
answers (violating Quantity), respond off-topic (Relation), or misinterpret ambiguous intent (Manner). These outcomes
are not necessarily “bad” but rather reflective of the model’s training data, lack of world knowledge, or inability
to interpret non-verbal cues. A study comparing various LLM generations noted a steady improvement in pragmatic handling,
suggesting that training scale and model design significantly affect performance \cite{bojic_gpt-4_2023}. Yet, challenges still remain,
such as repeated pragmatic failures lead users to simplify their speech over time, which may erode nuanced communication habits
\cite{eragamreddy_impact_2025}.

Despite advances in natural language processing and the increasing presence of conversational agents in daily life,
most AI systems still struggle with pragmatic competence, which is the ability to interpret and generate language that
is appropriate to social context. While these systems can produce grammatically correct and coherent responses, they
often fail to grasp implied meanings, respond appropriately to indirect speech acts, or adapt to the relational dynamics
of a conversation. This limitation becomes more noticeable in one-on-one interactions where subtle social cues and contextual
relevance are critical. This raises important questions: Can a chatbot be designed to recognize and respond to pragmatic
violations? How can feedback be delivered in a way that helps users reflect on and improve their communication strategies?
In turn, these questions highlight an opportunity to bridge the gap between linguistic fluency and social competence
by developing a chatbot that supports pragmatic learning through context-sensitive interactions and theory-informed
feedback.

\section{Research Objectives}

This study aims to develop a one-on-one chatbot that helps young adults ages 15 to 24 years old improve their social communication skills. Unlike task-based chatbots, it will simulate natural conversations, provide real-time feedback, and offer a safe space for practicing social interactions. The objectives of the study are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item To develop a chatbot grounded in the APAM framework that simulates natural one-on-one conversations as an AI Partner, enabling users to interact in contexts requiring pragmatic awareness.
    \item To integrate reflective feedback mechanisms that help users identify and improve their use of pragmatic strategies, including adherence to Grice’s Maxims and recognition of implicatures.
    \item To design realistic contexts and situations that young adults are likely to encounter in academic, professional, and social environments, providing them with opportunities to practice and refine their pragmatic skills.
    \item To evaluate the chatbot’s effectiveness in enhancing users' pragmatic competence and social communication skills through user feedback.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The chatbot will be developed using the APAM framework, emphasizing situation emulation and the provision of feedback. Its capacity to deliver pragmatic guidance will rely on training with the GRICE dataset and machine learning models, rather than on human-like comprehension. Accordingly, the effectiveness of the system is dependent on the alignment between the training data and the Gricean maxims and implicatures outlined in this study.
    \item The primary scope of this feedback is its direct alignment with Grice’s Maxims (Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner) and the recognition of implicatures, serving as the explicit framework for assessing and guiding user improvement. A key delimitation here is that the feedback's effectiveness is contingent on the chatbot's accurate identification of pragmatic violations or successes, which can be challenging given the inherent ambiguity of human communication and the limitations of current natural language processing (NLP). The system will provide feedback based on pre-defined pragmatic rules and patterns, rather than nuanced, context-specific human judgment. A potential gap is that while the feedback targets Grice's Maxims and implicatures, it may not comprehensively cover all facets of pragmatic competence, such as politeness strategies or speech act theory, which are outside the immediate scope of measurement for this particular intervention.
    \item The study will define conversational scenarios that reflect academic, professional, and casual social contexts relevant to young adults. This is best practiced since these are general situations that young adults would encounter everyday, most especially that they would build connections or relationships within these environments. These scenarios will serve as structured environments for practicing pragmatic competence. These situations will be constructed based the situations present in the GRICE dataset, wherein Grice's maxims and implicatures are implemented. The focus of the chatbot is on verbal communication, thereby the conversations presented in a text-based format would reflect real-world conversations. 
    \item The evaluation of the chatbot will be conducted through user interaction data and self-reported feedback. The assessment will focus on the user's perception on the chatbot's ability to simulate contextually appropriate conversation whilst providing pragmatically-aligned feedback after the conversation. Thus, this is based on the user's self-reported experience on the conversation authenticity.  
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology}
\indent To achieve the objectives of this study, the methodology involves the design, development, and evaluation of a pragmatically-aware chatbot system grounded in the APAM (AI Partner–AI Mentor) framework. The methodology is structured into four major phases: (1) Building the Knowledge Base, (2) Software Development, (3) User Data Collection, and (4) Validation.

\subsection{Knowledge Base Construction}
\indent The chatbot's pragmatics training capability lies in a properly curated and theory-driven knowledge base. The GRICE dataset, which consists of dialogue examples structured around Grice’s Maxims, will be the foundation with which the chatbot will be trained on. Each dialogue is designed to highlight either adherence to or violation of these maxims, providing concrete examples of pragmatic competence. \subsection{Software Development}
\indent To supplement the dataset, additional conversational prompts were generated using GPT-4 with explicit prompts to align with the same pragmatic structures. The dataset is found in a public GitHub repository, making it open source while additional dialogues or scenarios will be synthetically generated by AI. This ensures that there are no copyright or licensing concerns.

\subsection{Software Development}
\indent The chatbot, named Socius, will be implemented using the Python programming language, and will be integrating GPT-4 through the use of an API. This chatbot will follow a conversational loop that consists of four stages: (1) Scenario Selection, (2) Conversation Simulation, (3) Evaluation, and (4) Reflection or Retry.  Each stage is designed to assess the user’s pragmatic competence, specifically, their adherence to Grice’s Maxims based on different social scenarios that the chatbot will generate. The chatbot will assume two roles simultaneously, an AI Partner for practicing conversation skills, and an AI Mentor that provides feedback.

\subsection{User Data Collection}
\indent In order to measure the performance of the chatbot, data will be gathered from volunteer participants, consisting of young adults aged 15-24 years old. The users will interact with the chatbot, and these interactions will be stored as chat logs which will be used for analysis of user progress, pragmatic competence, and conversational behavior. Before engaging with the study, participants must fill out an informed consent form. Participants that are younger than 18 years old will have to fill out a parental consent and participant assent form. User data will be anonymized and used solely for research purposes. The data will be immediately destroyed once the study completes.
