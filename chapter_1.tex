\chapter{1. Introduction}\label{sec:intro}


\section{Background of the Study}

\indent Pragmatics is a subfield of linguistics that examines the contribution of context to the meaning of a message.
Unlike grammar or semantics, which focus on structural correctness and literal interpretation,
pragmatics deals with the intention, implication, and appropriateness of a statement during social interactions. It involves
going beyond the surface level, looking for the implied meaning rather than the literal meaning. It is essentially how speakers navigate relevance,
politeness, and indirectness depending on social context \cite{marasabessy_pragmatic_2023}. Individuals experiencing difficulties with pragmatics 
are more susceptible to instances of miscommunication or social isolation.

Many individuals struggle with real-world social interactions, yet there is a lack of accessible, judgment-free spaces to practice and refine 
their communication skills. Despite recent advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Large Language Models (LLMs), their potential for 
supporting social communication training remains underexplored. Most existing chatbots are not designed to promote adaptive and context-sensitive 
conversation practice, which leaves a critical gap in available tools. A chatbot focused on enhancing pragmatic competence designed for the general
population could provide a low-risk environment to build confidence and conversational fluency in individuals.

The need for pragmatics training is not limited to neurodivergent individuals. Pragmatics plays a vital role in grounding meaning through 
context \cite{fried_pragmatics_2023}, which is vital for effective communication and conversation. This highlights the broader application of pragmatics 
training as a way to improve real-world communicative competence across diverse settings. Furthermore,\cite{gibbs_pragmatics_2020} argue that 
pragmatic performance is influenced by cultural expectations, personal histories, and situational variability. These are factors that affect all individuals,
regardless of diagnostic status. Their work suggests that understanding and practicing pragmatic nuance can lead to more meaningful and effective 
social engagement across the board, thereby maximizing its potential benefit and broad applicability.

Recent work in social skill training has introduced the AI Partner and AI Mentor (APAM) framework as a promising model
for combining simulated practice and pedagogical feedback using LLMs \cite{yang_social_2024}.
In this framework, the AI Partner engages the user in realistic, domain-specific conversational scenarios, serving as 
a practice partner that reflects varied social contexts. The AI Mentor, on the other hand, observes the interaction and 
provides theory-grounded feedback, contextual suggestions, and reflective insights to promote social learning. This
dual-agent structure is particularly effective for training pragmatic competence, as it allows users to explore 
conversational nuance while receiving guidance grounded in expert frameworks like Grice’s Maxims. The APAM framework 
supports scalable, low-risk social communication training and has been applied to domains such as counseling, education,
and conflict resolution. In this study, APAM is adapted to focus specifically on one-on-one, pragmatic communication, 
with the AI Partner simulating conversations that test the user’s ability to navigate implicature and context, and the
AI Mentor offering reflective feedback based on violations or observance of conversational maxims.

Grice's Cooperative Principle is a key theoretical framework for pragmatic analysis. It includes four maxims: Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner. 
These maxims guide how speakers are expected to contribute to conversations, which is by being informative, truthful, relevant, and clear \cite{hossain_application_2021}. 
When these maxims are followed, communication tends to proceed smoothly. Should violations occur, intentional or not, these may lead to confusion, implicature, or breakdowns. 

Large Language Models like GPT-4 and Gemini 3 have been evaluated on Gricean tasks and, in some cases, they outperform humans in identifying implicature or 
recovering unstated meaning \mbox{\cite{bojic_gpt-4_2023}}. However, achieving true pragmatic competence in an educational tool requires more than recognizing 
logical maxims. It also demands an understanding of developmental psychology and cultural context. For young adults navigating the transition to adulthood, 
conversational challenges are deeply tied to identity exploration and changing social expectations \cite{arnett_emerging_2000,havighurst_developmental_1948}. 
Furthermore, in the Philippine context, communication is heavily shaped by cultural values such as 
\textit{Pakikisama} (smooth interpersonal relations), \textit{Hiya} (sense of shame or face), \textit{Utang na Loob} (debt of gratitude), and \textit{Paggalang} (respect) \cite{jocano_filipino_1997}.

In practical chatbot interactions, these maxims are regularly tested. For example, an LLM might provide overly verbose
answers (violating Quantity), respond off-topic (Relation), or misinterpret ambiguous intent (Manner). These outcomes
are not necessarily “bad” but are rather reflective of the model’s training data, lack of world knowledge, or inability
to interpret non-verbal cues. A study comparing various LLM generations noted a steady improvement in pragmatic handling,
suggesting that training scale and model design significantly affect performance \cite{bojic_gpt-4_2023}. Yet, challenges still remain,
such as repeated pragmatic failures leading users to simplify their speech over time, which may erode nuanced communication habits
\cite{eragamreddy_impact_2025}.

Despite advances in natural language processing and the increasing presence of conversational agents in daily life,
most AI systems still struggle with pragmatic competence, or the ability to interpret and generate language that
is appropriate to a social context. While these systems can produce grammatically correct and coherent responses, they
often fail to grasp implied meanings, respond appropriately to indirect speech acts, or adapt to the relational dynamics
of a conversation. This limitation becomes more noticeable in one-on-one interactions where subtle social cues and contextual
relevance are critical. This raises important questions: Can a chatbot be designed to recognize and respond to pragmatic
violations? How can feedback be delivered in a way that helps users reflect on and improve their communication strategies?
These questions highlight an opportunity to bridge the gap between linguistic fluency and social competence
by developing a chatbot that supports pragmatic learning through context-sensitive interactions and theory-informed
feedback.

While current chatbot applications focus more on therapeutic services, such as Woebot \cite{Woebot:2025}, there is a lack of tools designed for non-therapeutic 
needs, such as for enhancing social communication skills. Moreover, many young adults (ages 18–24) struggle with real-world social interactions but lack accessible, 
judgment-free spaces to practice. Existing chatbots are not designed for adaptive social communication training, often failing to accommodate diverse 
communication styles or provide meaningful, real-time feedback.

\section{Research Objectives}

\indent This study aims to develop a one-on-one chatbot that helps young adults ages 18 to 24 years old improve their social communication skills. 
Unlike task-based chatbots, it will simulate natural conversations, provide real-time feedback, and offer a safe space for practicing social interactions. 
The objectives of the study are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item To design and implement a dual-agent architecture (AI Partner and AI Mentor) utilizing the Google Gemini API to facilitate a natural roleplay experience and deep pragmatic analysis.
    \item To formulate a theoretical framework for scenario generation that synthesizes Grice's Cooperative Principle, Developmental Psychology (Havighurst and Arnett), and Filipino Cultural Values.
    \item To develop a multi-level, quantitative scoring rubric for pragmatic competence—enforced via JSON Schema Controlled Generation—that provides detailed, actionable metrics across Intention Coherence, Social \& Affective Nuance, and Communicative Effectiveness.
    \item To evaluate the chatbot's effectiveness in enhancing users' pragmatic competence and social communication skills through user feedback and human user testing.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Limitations}
\indent The chatbot was developed using the AI Partner - AI Mentor framework, emphasizing situation emulation and the provision of personalized feedback. 
The primary scope of this feedback encompasses a multi-level scoring framework assessing three core domains: Intention Coherence (logical flow and speech acts), 
Social \& Affective Nuance (formality, empathy, and informativeness), and Communicative Effectiveness (goal achievement and implicature handling). While grounded in 
Grice's Maxims (Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner), the system expands upon them to include developmental psychology and cultural pragmatics. Moreover, these 
maxims and implicatures are not reliant on paralinguistic cues, making them suitable for implementation in a text-based AI system \cite{dale_role_1996}. 

In line with goal-oriented language generation, the chatbot relies on the reasoning ability of the Gemini 3 model family rather than a rigidly fine-tuned local model. 
Feedback is strictly enforced through a JSON Output Schema via the Gemini API, ensuring machine-parsable, quantitative metrics for longitudinal tracking without sacrificing 
natural language understanding. A potential limitation is that the system relies on the LLM's probabilistic evaluation of pragmatic violations rather than nuanced, 
context-specific human judgment. Furthermore, while the system effectively captures Gricean maxims and basic Filipino cultural values 
(e.g., \textit{Pakikisama}, \textit{Hiya}, \textit{Paggalang}), it may not comprehensively cover all facets of non-verbal or highly localized sociolinguistic phenomena.

The study defined four core conversational themes reflecting academic, professional, and casual social contexts relevant to young adults: Identity vs. Expectation, 
Autonomy vs. Dependence, Ambiguity vs. Definition, and Group vs. Self. To ensure relevance to the local context, explicit ``negative constraints" and conversational 
marker rules were injected into the LLM's system instructions. This forced the AI to utilize appropriate Taglish (a mix of Tagalog and English) and culturally 
specific emotional code-switching without breaking character or providing unnatural English translations.

Careful review and adaptation were carried out to preserve the original pragmatic intent while making the content accessible and 
relatable to Filipino users. The focus of the chatbot is on verbal communication, thereby the conversations presented in a text-based format would reflect real-world 
conversations. The target participants also strictly involved young adults aged 18 - 24. These participants need not be considered neurodivergent. 
This decision is based on the principle that pragmatic competence is a universal and developmental skill set, not exclusively a challenge for individuals with diagnosed 
conditions. The study’s goal is to create a broadly applicable educational tool for enhancing social communication, rather than a clinical intervention for a specific population.

The evaluation of the chatbot was conducted through user interaction data and self-reported feedback. The assessment focused on the user's perception of the chatbot's 
ability to simulate contextually appropriate conversation whilst providing pragmatically-aligned feedback after the conversation. 
Thus, this is based on the user's self-reported experience on the conversation authenticity. It is important to note that limitations may arise due to 
cultural differences between the base LLM's training data and the target population. Some participants may not be able to fully relate to 
or understand certain thematic conflicts, especially if the scenario is not appropriate for their age group. These potential mismatches 
may affect user engagement or interpretation and will be considered in the analysis of user feedback to inform future improvements.

\section{Significance of the Study}

\indent This study offers a theory-informed chatbot that incorporates pragmatic and conversational linguistics, particularly Grice's Maxims and the APAM framework, 
into an interactive training tool for students to develop their social communication skills. 
The key output is a dual-role system: an AI Partner that engages in one-on-one conversations grounded in real-world scenarios, and an AI Mentor that evaluates the user's 
pragmatic performance and offers reflective feedback based on observed violations or adherence to conversational norms. As such, this study also contributes to the 
understanding and exploration of an AI-driven approach to teaching, in general, a sociolinguistic skill for young adults. 

Furthermore, a significant contribution of this research lies in its architectural findings regarding LLM deployment for pedagogical tasks. 
The study demonstrates that for complex, theory-driven communication coaching, the advanced reasoning and in-context learning capabilities of a prompt-engineered 
generalist model (Gemini 3 Flash) significantly outperforms smaller models fine-tuned on narrow, specialized datasets. Additionally, the introduction of a 
``Holistic Mentor" feature, which aggregates longitudinal data across multiple sessions via a Supabase backend, provides a novel approach to tracking and coaching 
user progression over time, rather than limiting support to isolated per-session feedback.

Another significance of this research is its potential to benefit individuals within the specified age range. Being able to engage in conversation is a vital skill as it 
affects an individual's personal relationships, casual and professional interactions, and self-confidence. Moreover, the results from this study will be pertinent to educators, 
counselors, and researchers in the field of linguistics as it provides insights into how AI can be used in an educational manner as a way of providing another avenue of 
learning for young adults. The chatbot would be able to provide a safe environment for young adults to practice their conversational ability without the need of a human 
partner, thereby being interactive, judgement-free, and low-risk. This may also be beneficial for those who struggle with face-to-face interactions, such as those with social anxiety or the 
socially awkward. By integrating social theories such as Grice’s Maxims and the APAM framework—into the chatbot’s design, this research contributes a structured, theory-based 
approach to teaching pragmatic competence. Specifically, the conversation flows developed in this study may serve as practical templates for future AI-driven tools aimed at 
improving real-world communication skills in educational and therapeutic contexts.

By equipping young individuals with the ability to communicate more effectively, this research holds broader implications for society at large. As pragmatic competence is 
crucial not only for personal growth but also for constructive social participation, enhancing this skill among the youth can lead to more socially-aware communities. 
In the Philippine context, where communication is often shaped by high-context norms, indirectness, and respect for social hierarchies, being rooted in cultural values such as 
Pakikisama, Hiya, and Utang na Loob, the ability to navigate pragmatic cues is especially vital \cite{jocano_filipino_1997}. By modeling these dynamics through 
the chatbot, this study helps Filipino youth engage more effectively in culturally appropriate interactions across educational and social domains.

\indent To achieve the objectives of this study, the methodology involves the design, development, and evaluation of a pragmatically-aware chatbot system grounded in the APAM 
(AI Partner–AI Mentor) framework. The methodology is structured into seven major phases: 
(1) Knowledge Base Construction, (2) Backend Development, (3) Frontend Development, (4) Expert Validation of Scenarios, 
(5) User Validation and Testing Period, (6) Data Analysis, and (7) Documentation and Report Writing.
The details of these activities are as follows:

\subsection {Theoretical Framework Formulation and Scenario Generation}
\indent Rather than relying exclusively on an existing static dataset, the chatbot's pragmatics training capability was built upon a multi-pillar theoretical framework. 
Scenarios were dynamically structured around four recurring developmental themes synthesized from Arnett's Theory of Emerging Adulthood and Havighurst's Developmental Tasks: 
(1) Identity vs. Expectation, (2) Autonomy vs. Dependence, (3) Ambiguity vs. Definition, and (4) Group vs. Self. Each scenario was explicitly coded with a defined conflict, 
a conversational formula, and a specific user goal. To ensure cultural resonance, the scenarios and AI instructions were injected with constraints mapping to 
Filipino cultural values (\textit{Pakikisama}, \textit{Hiya}, \textit{Utang na Loob}) \cite{jocano_filipino_1997}.

\subsection{Software Development}
\indent The chatbot, named Socius, is implemented using the Python programming language and integrates the Gemini LLM via its API. 
This exploratory use of Gemini aims to examine how its conversational capabilities align with the APAM framework and Gricean principles, without making prior assumptions about 
its effectiveness. The chatbot follows a six-stage conversational loop: (1) Session Initialization, (2) Scenario Selection and Instantiation, (3) Conversation Simulation, \
(4) Pragmatic Logging, (5) Conversation Analysis, and (6) Metric Generation.

Each stage is designed to assess the user’s pragmatic competence, specifically their adherence to Grice’s Maxims within various social scenarios generated and seeded through 
the system. During the conversation, the chatbot assumes the role of an AI Partner, adopting a context-specific persona to engage the user in conversation. After the exchange, it shifts 
to its AI Mentor role, where it analyzes the transcript, logs maxim violations, and evaluates the user’s responses based on relevance, informativeness, clarity, and recognition of 
implied meaning. The system then generates feedback and performance metrics, enabling reflection and, optionally, scenario retry to support iterative learning.

The stages of software development are as follows:

\subsubsection{Backend Development}
\indent Backend development focused on the core conversational engine built using Python and Flask, integrating the \texttt{google-genai} SDK to handle response generation. 
The backend managed session isolation, scenario selection, and database operations using Supabase. Crucially, the development included the implementation of the 
Pragmatic Analyzer via JSON Schema Controlled Generation, ensuring feedback was structured into actionable numerical scores and text summaries rather than relying on 
generic text generation.

\subsubsection{Frontend Development}
\indent Frontend development focused on creating the User Interface (UI). The UI was designed as a 
text-messaging application to provide a familiar and intuitive user experience for text-based interaction and feedback delivery. Key features include a 
chat window for real-time conversation with the chatbot and a component for selecting from predefined social scenarios. The interface presents 
alternating message colors and timestamps to clearly distinguish between user and chatbot messages. The development also includes 
a user profile section where users can track their progress through the Holistic Mentor dashboard across multiple sessions. 
The design prioritizes usability and accessibility through simple graphical elements.

\subsection{Expert Validation and Architectural Pivot}
\indent The conversational scenarios generated for the chatbot underwent expert validation. 
These scenarios were generated using Gemini 3.0 Pro and were designed by the researchers to reflect the theoretical 
framework of Grice's Maxims and Implicatures. Thus, it is important to ensure that these scenarios are relevant, culturally appropriate, and aligned with the intended learning 
outcomes. To accomplish this, an expert in the philosophy of language, Dr. Joseph M. Jose, PhD, an Assistant Professorial Lecturer from 
De La Salle University - Manila's Department of Philosophy, was consulted to review the scenarios that have been constructed based on the 
GRICE dataset \cite{zheng_grice_2021}. This validation process ensured that the scenarios are not only grounded in pragmatic theory but are also appropriate and relevant 
for the target demographic of young adults aged 18-24. The expert assessed whether the scenarios effectively emulated 
real-world academic, professional, and social contexts and whether the localized examples for the Filipino context maintain their intended pragmatic meaning. 
Only scenarios approved by the expert were added to the scenario database for use in the chatbot interactions.

Initial architectural experiments involved an attempt to fine-tune a smaller open-source model (Mistral-7B) on the GRICE dataset \mbox{\cite{zheng_grice_2021}} 
to serve as the AI Mentor. However, functional testing revealed that this approach yielded brittle, overly literal feedback that failed to capture cultural nuances or 
provide supportive pedagogical advice. Consequently, the system was successfully pivoted to a prompt-engineered architecture utilizing the superior reasoning capabilities 
of Gemini 3. The newly generated conversational scenarios and the multi-level scoring rubric underwent expert review to ensure they accurately reflected real-world academic, 
professional, and social contexts relevant to Filipino young adults aged 18-24, maintaining their intended pragmatic relevance without relying on the original rigid dataset.

\subsection{Validation and Testing Period}
\subsubsection{User Validation}
\indent User testing was conducted with 25 valid volunteer participants. Before engaging with the study, participants entered a two-part process. 
The initial stage was a need-finding study to confirm the need for a pragmatic skills training tool, and verify the target audience's interest and willingness to use the 
chatbot. This involved the distribution of a Pre-Assessment Form created using Google Forms.

\subsubsection{Participant Selection}
\indent The participant selection process began with recruiting volunteers from the target demographic of young adults aged 18 to 24. 
A convenience sampling method was employed, utilizing online announcements and social media channels to distribute the Pre-Assessment Form to a broad audience.
The primary inclusion criteria for the study were participants falling within the specified age range and who submitted their informed consent forms. 

From the pool of eligible respondents, a purposive sampling strategy was then used to select a target sample of 25 participants for the chatbot interaction 
phase. This purposive approach aimed to create a diverse cohort, representing a spectrum of self-reported communication confidence levels and varying degrees of interest in 
using a chatbot solution, as identified from their Pre-Assessment Form answers. This method ensured that the final participant group was not skewed toward a single profile but 
instead represented the broad audience for which Socius is designed.

\subsubsection{Pre-Assessment Form}
\indent The Pre-Assessment Form gathered demographic information such as age, name, and contact details, as well as the participant's experience with social 
communication and chatbots. It includes the following sections: (1) Introduction and Consent, (2) Participant's General Communication Confidence, 
(3) Participant's Pragmatic Skill Self-Assessment, (4) Attitudes towards a Chatbot-based Solution. This focused on gathering the participant's self-reported data on their communication habits, challenges, 
and their openness to a technological solution. 

\hl{The first section, Introduction and Consent, first clarified the study's objective to understand communication experiences and ensure the participant agreed to proceed 
by detailing all the necessary information about the study stated in the informed consent form that the participant must understand prior to consenting to the study. 
The form also reassured the participant that they were allowed to withdraw at any time within the study, and that their data would be anonymized and used solely for 
research purposes. The data would be immediately destroyed once the study is completed.}

\hl{The second section, Participant's General Communication Confidence, employed a 5-point Likert scale to establish a baseline of how comfortable participants feel in various common social contexts, 
such as speaking in groups or meeting new people.} 

\hl{The third section, Participant's Pragmatic Skill Self-Assessment, explored specific conversational behaviors related to pragmatic competence. This included self-perceived ability in areas such as providing an 
appropriate amount of information, staying on topic, and interpreting non-literal meanings like sarcasm or indirect hints.}

\hl{The last section, Attitudes towards a Chatbot-based Solution, directly assessed the need and interest in the Socius chatbot. This part gauged how likely the participant would use such a tool, what their primary 
concerns might be (e.g., privacy, effectiveness), and what features they would find most valuable.}

\subsubsection{Orientation Session}
\indent \hl{Once the final cohort of 25 participants was selected, each individual was invited to a Discord server, where a welcoming message was sent. This message included a thank you note for their participation,
a reiteration of the study's duration, as well as a short FAQ section to address possible questions regarding the chatbot interaction process. Once the participants notified the Socius team of their availability, 
they would undergo the orientation session, which was conducted through a voice channel in Discord. The purpose was to ensure all participants began their interaction with a clear and consistent understanding of 
what they were expected to do. The content of the orientation included:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Brief reiteration of the purpose of the study.
\item Provided clear instructions on how to access and log in to the Socius chatbot.
\item Outlined the expected level of participation (e.g., \hl{"Please engage with one conversational scenario over five days."}).
\item Informed them that their (anonymized) conversation data would be logged for analysis and that they would be asked to complete a final survey after the interaction period.
\item \hl{Stated that they must contact at least one researcher before the interaction period to accompany them in the voice channel in case they encounter technical difficulties or have questions.}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Chatbot Interaction Period}
\indent Following the orientation, the main experimental phase will begin, lasting for a predefined period of five (5) days. During this time, participants were free to 
interact with the Socius chatbot at their own convenience. Their primary task was to engage in conversations within the scenarios provided by the chatbot,
practicing their pragmatic communication skills in a simulated environment. All interactions, including the participant's inputs and the chatbot's responses, 
were automatically and anonymously logged in the backend database for subsequent analysis.

\subsubsection{Post-Interaction Survey}
\indent To gather data on participants' experiences and perceptions of the chatbot, they were told to answer a post interaction survey at the end of each day. 
This recurring survey served as the primary instrument for collecting timely feedback on usability, learning, and engagement. The form was designed to be concise 
and to capture quantitative data through 5-point Likert scale questions assessing the chatbot's \hl{Performance (scenario immersion, roleplay, advice/feedback quality), 
Humanity (ability to communicate in a human-like manner), and Affect (enjoyment, perceived helpfulness of social situation emulation and pragmatic feedback, and interest in using the chatbot again).}

\subsubsection{\hl{Consolidated Feedback Form}}
\indent \hl{After the five-day interaction period, participants were asked to complete a final survey that consolidated their overall experience with the chatbot. 
This included questions asking about the following:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \hl{Overall Experience}
\item \hl{Perceived Learning and Change}
\item \hl{Emotional Safety}
\item \hl{Response Quality}
\item \hl{Usability and Technical Reliability}
\item \hl{Likelihood to Recommend the Chatbot to Others}
\item Additional Comments or Suggestions for Improvement
\end{itemize}

\hl{This final survey provided a comprehensive overview of the participant's experience and allowed for the collection of additional qualitative data for analysis of the chatbot's effectiveness 
and areas for future enhancement.}

\subsection{\hl{Data Analysis}}
\indent \hl{Both quantitative and qualitative testing were done to assess the usability and effectiveness of Socius. This involved the analysis of both the participants' chat logs as well as their responses to the
post-interaction surveys and consolidated feedback form.}

\hl{The quantitative analysis was done through the use of a Wilcoxon-signed rank test which was performed on each of the three metrics that users were measured on by the chatbot in order to determine if there was a 
significant change in the users' pragmatic competence after using the chatbot. The three metrics were as follows:} 
\begin{itemize}
\item \hl{Intention Coherence - ability to communicate intent clearly and consistently}
\item \hl{Social \& Affective Nuance - ability to understand and appropriately respond to social cues, emotional undertones, and cultural norms in conversation, and}
\item \hl{Communicative Effectiveness - ability to achieve conversational goals, such as providing relevant information, maintaining engagement, and successfully navigating implicatures.}
\end{itemize}

On the other hand, the qualitative analysis focused on the participants' subjective experiences and perceptions of the chatbot. This involved analyzing their responses to the post-interaction surveys and 
consolidated feedback form, where common themes, insights, and areas for improvement were identified.

\subsection{Documentation and Report Writing}
\indent The final phase of the methodology involves documenting the development process, user interactions, data analysis, and validation results.
This will include a comprehensive report detailing the design choices, implementation challenges, and insights gained from user
interactions. The report will also include recommendations for future improvements and potential applications of the chatbot in
educational contexts.
