\section{Background of the Study}

Pragmatics, a subfield of linguistics, discusses how context contributes to meaning in communication.
Pragmatics, unlike grammar or semantics, which focus on structural correctness and literal interpretation, 
is more involved with intention, implication, and appropriateness within social interactions. It involves 
understanding what is meant beyond what is said; meaning over literal. In essence, it is how speakers navigate relevance,
politeness, and indirectness depending on social context \cite{marasabessy2023}. For individuals with social communication
challenges, such as those with neurodivergence or social anxiety, difficulties in pragmatic interpretation may result in 
social isolation or miscommunication.

As user-interaction with machine becomes more prevalent, artificial intelligence (AI) systems are increasingly implemented
with simulating pragmatically appropriate communication. This challenge has caused an increase in research in Applied
Pragmatics for Artificial Minds (APAM), a framework that integrates pragmatic reasoning into AI, particularly chatbots
and virtual companions. This framework is concerned with enabling conversational agents to respect social norms, recognize 
intention, and adapt communication according to context. For example, ChatGPT was evaluated for its use of speech acts like 
apologies and refusals, revealing inconsistnecies in how it adjusts strategies based on imposition \cite{lee2024}. Similarly, 
AI systems still struggle with implicit meaning, indirect speech, and culutral nuance - pragmatic features that humans process
naturally \cite{eragamreddy2025}.

Grice's Cooperative Priniciple is a key theoretical framework for pragmatic analysis. It includes four maxims: Quantity,
Quality, Relation, and Manner. These maxims guide how pseakers are expected to contribute to conversations, which is by 
being informative, truthful, relevant, and clear \cite{hossain2021}. When these maxims are followed, communication
tends to proceed smmothly. However, vioaltions, intentional or not, may lead to confusion, implicature, or breakdowns.
Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4 have been evaluated on Gricean tasks and, in some cases, outperform
humans in identifying implicature or recovering unstated meaning \cite{bojic2023}. Datasets such as GRICE have even
been created to assess AI's ability to handle nuanced conversational reasoning \cite{zheng2021}.

In practical chatbot interactions, these maxims are regularly tested. For example, an LLM might provide overly verbose
answers (violating Quantity), respond off-topic (Relation), or misinterpret ambiguous intent (Manner). These outcomes
are not necessarily “bad” but rather reflective of the model’s training data, lack of world knowledge, or inability
to interpret non-verbal cues. A study comparing various LLM generations noted a steady improvement in pragmatic handling,
suggesting that training scale and model design significantly affect performance \cite{bojic2023}. Yet, challenges still remain,
such as repeated pragmatic failures lead users to simplify their speech over time, which may erode nuanced communication habits
\cite{eragamreddy2025}.

Given this, this study proposes a pragmatically-aware, one-on-one chatbot designed to support social communication
practice. Rather than focusing on transactional or task-oriented dialogue, the system is intended to simulate natural,
open-ended conversations where users can practice responses, receive feedback, and experience varied situational prompts
based on Gricean analysis. Performance will not be judged as successful or not in absolute terms; instead, it will be
examined through the lens of pragmatic coherence — whether the chatbot’s output aligns with contextual expectations,
and why certain (mis)interpretations occur.
