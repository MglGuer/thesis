\chapter{1. Introduction}\label{sec:intro}


\section{Background of the Study}

\indent Pragmatics, a subfield of linguistics, discusses how context contributes to meaning in communication.
Pragmatics, unlike grammar or semantics, which focus on structural correctness and literal interpretation,
is more involved with intention, implication, and appropriateness within social interactions. It involves
understanding what is meant beyond what is said; implied meaning over literal meaning. In essence, it is how speakers navigate relevance,
politeness, and indirectness depending on social context \cite{marasabessy_pragmatic_2023}. For individuals with social communication
challenges, difficulties in pragmatic interpretation may result in social isolation or miscommunication.

\hl{As such, many individuals struggle with real-world social interactions yet lack accessible, judgment-free spaces to practice and refine 
these essential skills. Despite recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and large language models (LLMs), their potential for 
supporting social communication training remains underexplored. Most existing chatbots are not designed to promote adaptive, context-sensitive 
conversation practice, leaving a critical gap in available tools. A chatbot focused on enhancing pragmatic competence—especially for the general
population—could serve as a low-risk environment to build confidence and conversational fluency.}

\hl{Crucially, the need for pragmatics training is not limited to neurodivergent individuals. Pragmatics plays a vital role in grounding meaning through 
context} \cite{fried_pragmatics_2023}, \hl{which is crucial for effective communication and conversation. This highlights the broader applicability of pragmatic 
training as a way to improve real-world communicative competence across diverse settings. Furthermore,}\cite{gibbs_pragmatics_2020} \hl{argue that 
pragmatic performance is influenced by cultural expectations, personal histories, and situational variability—factors that affect all individuals,
regardless of diagnostic status. Their work suggests that understanding and practicing pragmatic nuance can foster more meaningful and effective 
social engagement across the board, thereby maximizing its potential benefit and broad applicability.}

Recent work in social skill training has introduced the AI Partner and AI Mentor (APAM) framework as a promising model
for combining simulated practice and pedagogical feedback using large language models (LLMs) \cite{yang_social_2024}.
In this framework, the AI Partner engages the user in realistic, domain-specific conversational scenarios, serving as 
a practice partner that reflects varied social contexts. The AI Mentor, on the other hand, observes the interaction and 
provides theory-grounded feedback, contextual suggestions, and reflective insights to promote social learning. This
dual-agent structure is particularly effective for training pragmatic competence, as it allows users to explore 
conversational nuance while receiving guidance grounded in expert frameworks like Grice’s Maxims. The APAM framework 
supports scalable, low-risk social communication training and has been applied to domains such as counseling, education,
and conflict resolution. In this study, APAM is adapted to focus specifically on one-on-one, pragmatic communication, 
with the AI Partner simulating conversations that test the user’s ability to navigate implicature and context, and the
AI Mentor offering reflective feedback based on violations or observance of conversational maxims.

Grice's Cooperative Priniciple is a key theoretical framework for pragmatic analysis. It includes four maxims: Quantity,
Quality, Relation, and Manner. These maxims guide how speakers are expected to contribute to conversations, which is by 
being informative, truthful, relevant, and clear \cite{hossain_application_2021}. When these maxims are followed, communication
tends to proceed smmothly. However, vioaltions, intentional or not, may lead to confusion, implicature, or breakdowns.
Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4 have been evaluated on Gricean tasks and, in some cases, outperform
humans in identifying implicature or recovering unstated meaning \cite{bojic_gpt-4_2023}. Datasets such as GRICE have even
been created to assess AI's ability to handle nuanced conversational reasoning \cite{zheng_grice_2021}.

In practical chatbot interactions, these maxims are regularly tested. For example, an LLM might provide overly verbose
answers (violating Quantity), respond off-topic (Relation), or misinterpret ambiguous intent (Manner). These outcomes
are not necessarily “bad” but rather reflective of the model’s training data, lack of world knowledge, or inability
to interpret non-verbal cues. A study comparing various LLM generations noted a steady improvement in pragmatic handling,
suggesting that training scale and model design significantly affect performance \cite{bojic_gpt-4_2023}. Yet, challenges still remain,
such as repeated pragmatic failures lead users to simplify their speech over time, which may erode nuanced communication habits
\cite{eragamreddy_impact_2025}.

Despite advances in natural language processing and the increasing presence of conversational agents in daily life,
most AI systems still struggle with pragmatic competence, or the ability to interpret and generate language that
is appropriate to social context. While these systems can produce grammatically correct and coherent responses, they
often fail to grasp implied meanings, respond appropriately to indirect speech acts, or adapt to the relational dynamics
of a conversation. This limitation becomes more noticeable in one-on-one interactions where subtle social cues and contextual
relevance are critical. This raises important questions: Can a chatbot be designed to recognize and respond to pragmatic
violations? How can feedback be delivered in a way that helps users reflect on and improve their communication strategies?
These questions highlight an opportunity to bridge the gap between linguistic fluency and social competence
by developing a chatbot that supports pragmatic learning through context-sensitive interactions and theory-informed
feedback.

While current chatbot applications focus more on therapeutic services, such as Woebot \cite{Woebot:2025}, there is a lack of tools designed for non-therapeutic 
needs, such as for enhancing social communication skills. Moreover, Many young adults (15–24) struggle with real-world social interactions but lack accessible, 
judgment-free spaces to practice. Existing chatbots are not designed for adaptive social communication training, often failing to accommodate diverse 
communication styles or provide meaningful, real-time feedback.

\section{Research Objectives}

\indent This study aims to develop a one-on-one chatbot that helps young adults ages 15 to 24 years old improve their social communication skills. Unlike task-based chatbots, it will simulate natural conversations, provide real-time feedback, and offer a safe space for practicing social interactions. The objectives of the study are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item To develop a chatbot grounded in the APAM framework that simulates natural one-on-one conversations as an AI Partner, enabling users to interact in contexts requiring pragmatic awareness.
    \item To design realistic contexts and situations that young adults are likely to encounter in academic, professional, and social environments, providing them with opportunities to practice and refine their pragmatic skills.
    \item To evaluate the chatbot’s effectiveness in enhancing users' pragmatic competence and social communication skills through user feedback.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Limitations}
\indent The chatbot will be developed using the APAM framework, emphasizing situation emulation and the provision of feedback. The primary scope of this feedback is its direct alignment with Grice’s Maxims (Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner) and the recognition of implicatures, serving as the explicit framework for assessing and guiding user improvement. Moreover, these maxims and implicatures are not reliant on paralinguistic cues, making them suitable for implementation in a text-based AI system \cite{dale_role_1996}. In line with goal-oriented language generation, the chatbot’s responses are designed to reflect Gricean principles without the need to explicitly enforce them as rigid rules. Its capacity to deliver pragmatic guidance will rely on training with the GRICE dataset and machine learning models, providing feedback based on pre-defined pragmatic rules rather than on human-like comprehension or nuanced, context-specific human judgment. Accordingly, the effectiveness of the system is dependent on the alignment between the training data and the Gricean maxims, and the feedback’s effectiveness is contingent on the chatbot's accurate identification of pragmatic violations or successes, a task made challenging by the inherent ambiguity of human communication and the limitations of current natural language processing (NLP). A potential gap is that while the feedback targets Grice's Maxims and implicatures, it may not comprehensively cover all facets of pragmatic competence, such as politeness strategies or speech act theory, which are outside the immediate scope of measurement for this particular intervention.

The study will define conversational scenarios that reflect academic, professional, and casual social contexts relevant to young adults. This is best practiced since these are general situations that young adults would encounter everyday, most especially that they would build connections or relationships within these environments. These scenarios will serve as structured environments for practicing pragmatic competence. These situations will be constructed based the situations present in the GRICE dataset, wherein Grice's maxims and implicatures are implemented. \hl{However, in order to ensure relevance to the local context as Grice's theory of conversation is primarily a western theory, select examples from the GRICE dataset will be localized by modifying culturally specific references and language usage to better reflect local Filipino cultural norms and social dynamics. Careful review and adaptation will be carried out to preserve the original pragmatic intent while making the content accessible and relatable to Filipino users. The focus of the chatbot is on verbal communication, thereby the conversations presented in a text-based format would reflect real-world conversations. The target participants will also strictly involve young adults aged 15 - 24, moreover that these participants need not be considered neurodivergent. This decision is based on the principle that pragmatic competence is a universal and developmental skill set, not exclusively a challenge for individuals with diagnosed conditions. The study’s goal is to create a broadly applicable educational tool for enhancing social communication, rather than a clinical intervention for a specific population.}

The evaluation of the chatbot will be conducted through user interaction data and self-reported feedback. The assessment will focus on the user's perception on the chatbot's ability to simulate contextually appropriate conversation whilst providing pragmatically-aligned feedback after the conversation. Thus, this is based on the user's self-reported experience on the conversation authenticity. \hl{It is important to note that limitations may arise due to cultural differences between the original dataset and the target population. Filipino participants may not fully relate to or understand certain examples derived from the GRICE dataset, even after localization, especially if implicit meanings are grounded in unfamiliar cultural references. These potential mismatches may affect user engagement or interpretation and will be considered in the analysis of user feedback to inform future improvements.}  


% The study will limit its target demographic to those who are between 15 to 24 years old, aligning with the World Health Organization's (WHO, n.d.) definition of youth. This age range was chosen as young adults often face crucial transitions in academic, social, and professional settings where effective communication plays a key role. The chatbot will also be limited to text-based input and will not incorporate voice or video-based input. It will exclusively use the English language due to the availability of numerous datasets suitable for training. The study also aims to identify the different strategies used by experts in teaching communication skills to young adults, especially those who are deemed socially awkward. Furthermore, the chatbot itself will provide the situations and contexts that the user will experience, rather than having the context supplied by the user. In doing so, the study defines assistance strategies related to helping young adults with social awkwardness and pragmatics training that can be implemented in a chatbot. Moreover, the research will focus on the user's actual responses, particularly their word choices, rather than how they audibly deliver those responses. The study will also concentrate on the subtopic of social communication—specifically, pragmatics—which will be measured and assessed using Grice's Maxims and the concept of implicature.


\section{Significance of the Study}
% Placeholder text
\indent This study offers a theory-informed chatbot that incorporates pragmatic and conversational linguistics, particularly Grice's Maxims and the APAM framework, into an interactive training tool for students to develop their social communication skills. The key output is a dual-role system: an AI Partner that engages in one-on-one conversations grounded in real-world scenarios, and an AI Mentor that evaluates the user's pragmatic performance and offers reflective feedback based on observed violations or adherence to conversational norms. As such, this study's contribution is also the understanding and exploration of an AI-driven approach to teaching, in general, a sociolinguistic skill for young adults. 
Furthermore, In contrast to prior chatbot implementations using LLMs other than Gemini, this study explores the use of Gemini as an alternative large language model. The goal is not to evaluate its superiority, but to understand its potential to model pragmatic competence in an educational chatbot context.   

Another significance of this research is its potential to benefit individuals within a specific age range. Being able to engage in conversation is a vital skill as it affects one’s personal relationships, casual and professional interactions, and self-confidence. Moreover, the results from this study will be pertinent to educators, counselors, and researchers in the field of linguistics as it provides insights into how AI can be used in an educational manner as a way of providing another avenue of learning for young adults. The chatbot would be able to provide a safe environment for young adults to practice their conversational ability without the need of a human partner, thereby being interactive and low-risk. This may also be beneficial for those who struggle with face-to-face interactions, such as those with social anxiety or the socially awkward. By integrating social theories—such as Grice’s Maxims and the APAM framework—into the chatbot’s design, this research contributes a structured, theory-based approach to teaching pragmatic competence. Specifically, the conversation flows developed in this study may serve as practical templates for future AI-driven tools aimed at improving real-world communication skills in educational and therapeutic contexts.

By equipping young individuals with the ability to communicate more effectively, this research holds broader implications for society at large. As pragmatic competence is crucial not only for personal growth but also for constructive social participation, enhancing this skill among youth can lead to more socially-aware communities. In the Philippine context, where communication is often shaped by high-context norms, indirectness, and respect for social hierarchies, rooted in cultural values such as pakikisama, hiya, and utang na loob, the ability to navigate pragmatic cues is especially vital (Garcia, 2013; Nadar, 1999; Miranda, 1998). By modeling these dynamics through the chatbot, this study helps Filipino youth engage more effectively in culturally appropriate interactions across educational and social domains.

\section{Methodology}
\indent To achieve the objectives of this study, the methodology involves the design, development, and evaluation of a pragmatically-aware chatbot system grounded in the APAM (AI Partner–AI Mentor) framework. The methodology is structured into four major phases: (1) Building the Knowledge Base, (2) Software Development, (3) User Data Collection, and (4) Validation.

\subsection{Knowledge Base Construction}
\indent The chatbot's pragmatics training capability lies in a properly curated and theory-driven knowledge base. The GRICE dataset, which consists of dialogue examples structured around 
Grice’s Maxims, will be the foundation with which the chatbot will be trained on. Each dialogue is designed to highlight either adherence to or violation of these maxims, providing 
concrete examples of pragmatic competence. The scenarios constructed by the LLM will be validated by an expert before being stored in the scenario database for use by the chatbot.

To supplement the dataset, additional conversational prompts were generated using Gemini with explicit prompts to align with the same pragmatic structures. The dataset is found in a public 
GitHub repository, making it open source while additional dialogues or scenarios will be synthetically generated by AI. This ensures that there are no copyright or licensing concerns.

The GRICE dataset (Zheng et al., 2021), used in this research, is publicly available and was released as part of the Findings of ACL-IJCNLP 2021. It is © 2021 Association for Computational 
Linguistics. The dataset is used here strictly for academic and non-commercial research purposes, in accordance with ACL’s standard terms of use. No modifications or redistribution of the 
dataset are performed in this study.

\subsection{Software Development}
\indent \hl{The chatbot, named Socius, will be implemented using the Python programming language and will integrate the Gemini LLM via its API. 
This exploratory use of Gemini aims to examine how its conversational capabilities align with the APAM framework and Gricean principles, without making prior assumptions about 
its effectiveness. The chatbot will follow a six-stage conversational loop: (1) Session Initialization, (2) Scenario Selection and Instantiation, (3) Conversation Simulation, \
(4) Pragmatic Logging, (5) Conversation Analysis, and (6) Metric Generation.}

\hl{Each stage is designed to assess the user’s pragmatic competence, specifically their adherence to Grice’s Maxims within various social scenarios generated and seeded through the system. During the conversation, the chatbot assumes the role of an AI Partner, adopting a context-specific persona to engage the user in dialogue. After the exchange, it shifts to its AI Mentor role, where it analyzes the transcript, logs maxim violations, and evaluates the user’s responses based on relevance, informativeness, clarity, and recognition of implied meaning. The system then generates feedback and performance metrics, enabling reflection and, optionally, scenario retry to support iterative learning.}

\subsection{Validation}
\indent \hl{In order to validate the performance of the chatbot, data will be gathered from volunteer participants, consisting of young adults aged 15-24 years old.
The users will interact with the chatbot, and these interactions will be stored as chat logs which will be used for analysis of user progress, pragmatic 
competence, and conversational behavior. These chat logs will then be analyzed by an expert, either a behavioral scientist, or conversation expert, in order to assess whether the 
chatbot's responses are also pragmatically aligned. Before engaging with the study, participants must fill out an informed consent form. Minors must fill out a parents consent 
form and participant assent form. This form will detail the purpose of the study, procedures involved, potential risks and benefits, and the reassurance that the participant has 
the right to withdraw at any time. User data will be anonymized and used solely for research purposes. The data will be immediately destroyed once the study completes.}

\subsection{Data Analysis}
\indent Quantitative testing will be done to assess the usability and effectivity of Socius. This may include questions such as user experience, satisfaction, and chatbot authenticity, usefulness, and effectivity.
Additionally, the user will give their perceived improvement in their pragmatic competence as another factor of the chatbot's effectivity.

\subsection{Documentation and Report Writing}
\indent The final phase of the methodology involves documenting the development process, user interactions, data analysis, and validation results.
This will include a comprehensive report detailing the design choices, implementation challenges, and insights gained from user
interactions. The report will also include recommendations for future improvements and potential applications of the chatbot in
educational contexts.
