\chapter{Related Works}\label{sec:relatedworks}

\textbf{2.1 Chatbots Developed to Provide Support}

Conversational agents, or chatbots, have gained popularity across multiple platforms and fields, including mental health, education, and customer service. These AI-driven systems use natural language processing (NLP) to facilitate human-like interactions, providing real-time assistance to their users. Research has shown that chatbots can help lower communication barriers, particularly for individuals who experience social isolation, awkwardness, or difficulty accessing human support systems. Given their increasing role across various domains, it becomes critical to examine their applications in mental health and social communication, as these areas highlight the potential of chatbots in addressing emotional and interpersonal challenges. In this section, the ways in which chatbots are used to support mental health and enhance social communication skills are discussed.

Chatbots that provide support fall into two main categories: those that assist with mental health, and those that focus on social communication. Although their platforms may appear similar, their objectives differ. Social communication support chatbots are designed to improve interpersonal competence by simulating real-life conversations, offering roleplay-based exercises, and delivering structured feedback. These tools are especially beneficial for children and young adults who struggle with communication, as these difficulties are strongly associated with long-term mental health issues such as anxiety, depression, and behavioral disorders (Dall et al., 2022). Early interventions using chatbots to address these challenges may reduce the risk of social isolation and emotional distress by improving the ability to form meaningful social relationships.

Meanwhile, mental health-focused chatbots are developed to provide emotional and psychological support, targeting internal struggles like stress, anxiety, and depression. These systems, which often rely on frameworks such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), help users regulate their emotions and thought patterns. A prime example is Woebot, a chatbot that guides users through CBT-based exercises while conducting daily check-ins and offering psychoeducational content. In a study by Fitzpatrick et al. (2017), Woebot users showed significant reductions in depressive symptoms based on PHQ-9 scores. Although the chatbot showed high levels of engagement and accessibility, limitations such as repetitive responses and shallow understanding of user input were noted. Similarly, Therapist Vibe, introduced by Eren et al. (2020), uses storytelling prompts to help children articulate their emotions and reflect on solutions to social-emotional situations. The study found that children became more willing to discuss their feelings and gained a deeper understanding of emotional management. This demonstrates how chatbots can serve not only as support tools for mental health, but also as educational aids in emotional development.

Recent research has also examined the general acceptability of mental health chatbots. Koulouri, Macredi, and Olakitan (2022) found that young adults generally viewed chatbots as accessible and convenient solutions for emotional support. However, concerns about social stigma, poor design, and lack of personalization were highlighted. The study emphasized the importance of user-centered design to ensure effective, nonjudgmental experiences. Echoing these concerns, Haque and Rubya (2023) analyzed over 6,000 user reviews of popular mental health chatbots and found that while users appreciated the judgment-free environment, they also reported problems such as incorrect assumptions, unhelpful responses, and insufficient crisis response capabilities. Users often preferred the chatbots over speaking to friends or family, indicating the potential for overdependence and social withdrawal. While these tools can fill support gaps, the research also warned of the dangers of chatbots becoming a substitute for human care in situations where nuanced understanding or emergency assistance is needed.

Beyond emotional support, chatbots are also being explored as tools for training social communication skills. De Bever et al. (2019) developed two versions of a chatbot for training security guards—one that used both voice and text input, and another that used text only. While test scores did not vary significantly across groups, users reported that the chatbot was interactive and engaging. This suggests that chatbots can be effective supplementary tools in training environments where interpersonal communication is a critical component. Advancing this approach further is the AI Partner–AI Mentor (APAM) framework, which introduces a dual-agent system: an AI Partner that simulates realistic dialogue through roleplay, and an AI Mentor that provides reflective, personalized feedback. Though still conceptual, APAM outlines a four-step process to guide learners through iterative social skill development. These steps include identifying the target communication skill, simulating interactions, delivering targeted feedback, and allowing repeated practice. The framework emphasizes ethical considerations and design consistency, particularly as large language models (LLMs) are integrated into social skills training.

A real-world application of the AI Partner model can be seen in Duolingo’s roleplay feature, which provides users with scenarios to engage in chatbot conversations and receive feedback based on pragmatic appropriateness (Team D, 2023). This highlights how social communication training is less about grammatical correctness and more about selecting context-appropriate responses. Chatbots have also been shown to benefit individuals with social anxiety or autism spectrum disorder (ASD). According to Franze et al. (2023), AI systems like ChatGPT offer a safe, nonjudgmental environment for individuals with ASD to rehearse social interactions, build confidence, and practice conversational strategies. However, the same study cautioned against potential downsides such as user dependency, disengagement from real-world conversations, and reinforcement of conversational habits that are not socially effective—like dominating the dialogue or skipping responses. These findings suggest that while chatbots are a promising medium for social skills development, their design must be carefully moderated to avoid unintended consequences.highlighted how social chatbots can support individuals with social deficits, particularly those with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) or social anxiety. Franze et al. (2023) discuss how AI-driven chatbots such as ChatGPT, can provide a safe and nonjudgmental space for individuals with ASD to practice social interactions. These chatbots give the users the space to rehearse conversations, develop confidence, and receive real-time feedback without the fear of negative social consequences. However, the same study discusses the potential risks associated with chatbot use, including the reduced engagement in real-world interactions, user dependency on the chatbot, and reinforcement of dysfunctional social behaviors. Some chatbots, designed to be highly responsive to user input, may inadvertently encourage conversational habits that are unfavorable to real-world social communication, such as being in control of the entire dialogue, skipping responses, or fixating on a limited range of conversational interests.


\textbf{2.2 Other Technologies for Social Skills Training}

Other technologies were also leveraged to help improve social communication skills. Hoque et al. (2014) developed “My Automated Conversation Coach” or MACH, a fully automated nonverbal sensing system designed to improve social interaction skills. MACH interprets and provides real-time feedback to an individual’s nonverbal behavior. It tackles challenges in regards to the technology’s ability to recognize nonverbal cues, applications for social skills training, and potential future developments for automated feedback systems. A simulated job interview was done to assess and gauge the participants’ social skills, comparing the skills of those who used MACH, those who only reviewed MACH recordings, and those who didn’t use MACH. The use of MACH with its automated feedback resulted in a statistically significant improvement in social skills.

DeRosier et al. (2019) makes use of a game developed by 3C Institute called “Hall of Heroes,” which provides social skills training through a virtual story set in a superhero-themed world. Participants were split into two groups, one that played the game, and one that did not. Results showed a significant change in the experimental group’s interpersonal skills, with improvement in the ability to relate to others, accept affection, and express emotion. Additionally, there was also a significant decline in feelings of anxiety, depression, and hopelessness in the experimental group compared to the control group. Evaluations done by both the children and the parents suggest positive reception towards using the game for social skills training, indicating that video games can serve as an alternative and favorable approach towards SST. Similarly, game-mediated environments like multiuser virtual environments (MUVEs) have been shown to foster pragmatic competence by placing users in goal-driven, interactive digital contexts that require real-time communication and context-sensitive decision-making (Ko \& Eslami, 2021). These environments, including synthetic immersive environments (SIEs) and MMORPGs, allow learners to rehearse a wide range of speech acts in safe, low-anxiety spaces.

Recent developments in the virtual world also led to opportunities to make use of it in aiding with an individual’s social communication skills. A pilot study was conducted by Kolk et al. (2022) that involves a combination of multitouch-multiuser tabletops (MMT) and PowerVR, a virtual reality platform, and their use for training social communication skills for children with neurological disorders. The training sessions were split into one hour on the tabletop, and 30 minutes using VR technology once a week for ten weeks, with two therapists guiding them. Results showed improvements in the children’s ability to start conversations, as well as improvements in other social aspects such as empathy, conflict resolution, and being able to make friends. Qualitative results also showed positive reception towards the use of VR, with participants saying that its use is interesting, exciting, comfortable, and an innovative environment to learn and practice in various social situations.

Another technology used for social skills training comes from Lee et al. (2023), who investigates the use of metaverse-based social skills training programs to support children with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) in enhancing their social interaction abilities. Conducted as an open-label, single-center, randomized controlled pilot trial, the research explores how immersive virtual environments can serve as effective tools for social practice. The study highlights the need for personalization in chatbot-driven interactions and evaluates how participants respond to AI-generated feedback. By reviewing the opinions of participants, the study was able to underscore the importance of chatbot responsiveness and the quality of responses to facilitate effective social skills training. Moreover, the study suggests that future research should focus on refining chatbot content to improve user engagement.

\textbf{2.3} \textbf{Ethical Considerations in AI-Based Social Communication}

The use of artificial intelligence (AI) in social communication has given rise to a number of ethical concerns, particularly in the areas of bias, disinformation, mental health, education, and accessibility. As AI-powered chatbots continually get built and are integrated into society, it is vital to examine the ramifications of their use across different fields and avenues while stressing the necessity of fairness, openness, and human supervision to ensure moral implementation.

The potential for AI-driven chatbots, especially large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT, to inherently include social prejudices in their responses due to this being present in their training data is a significant ethical worry. According to Zhou et al. (2024), biased AI answers can perpetuate discrimination and false information thus calling for tactics like dataset audits and fairness-aware model training. Therefore, it is vital that the causalities of AI-decisions are made transparent to all users. This would ensure human-chatbot interactions would avoid bias and foster inclusivity. Moreover, Zhou et al. (2024) advocates for stricter AI ethics policies and frameworks that promote responsible and equitable chatbot interactions in reaction to the findings.

Despite the ease of access provided by chatbots when it comes to accessing support, it is also scrutinized for its dependence on preset scripts and the lack of contextual awareness. Thus, it has problems regarding the provided support’s accuracy, privacy, and possible misinformation (Abd-Alrazag et al., 2021). It is then important to stress that human supervision is required in order to avoid subjecting users to faulty support. Rather than used as a stand-alone tool, LLMs might be best used as a supplementary tool (Abd-Alrazag et al., 2021). Moreover, AI-generated content in educational settings has broader implications for learning experiences, requiring clear ethical guidelines to balance technological benefits with responsible use. 

In addition to this, Saylam et al., (2023) further talks about the challenges regarding AI’s role in education when it comes to academic integrity and critical thinking. The misuse of AI tools may impact their ability to develop their problem-solving skills, thereby increasing their dependency on tools instead of their own academic proficiency. Due to this, it is vital to propose policy frameworks that encourage ethical AI while reaping the benefits when it comes to efficiency and automation of the learning process (Saylam et al., 2023). It is also proposed by Saylam et al., (2023) that a learning environment utilizing and integrating AI could enhance educational outcomes, thus it is important to continually research this for the future. 

As we have assessed in a previous section, the use of AI to support individuals who are part of the spectrum (ASD) may be ethically problematic. Gagan et al. (2023) investigates the potential of AI-based narrative tools to assist in the development of social communication skills in children with Level 1 ASD. The improper implementation of AI to support those that are part of the spectrum, however, may lead to the individual having reduced human empathy, transparency loss, and dependence on users. Gagan et al. (2023) suggest a hybrid approach that overcomes these challenges by enabling AI systems to support human professionals instead of replacing them. This would then allow the implementation and deployment of AI in sensitive applications such as education and mental health.

In totality, the ethical challenges asociated with AI in social communication are related to the importance of transparency, fairness, and human collaboration to ensure that AI does not reinforce bias, compromise privacy, and diminish human empathy. This would then allow for their ethical and responsible deployment in different fields..

\textbf{2.4 Foundational Studies on Social Communication and Mental Health}

Foundational studies on social communication and mental health provide critical insights into how individuals perceive,
experience, and develop communicative competence across various contexts. For one, Guclu (2016) conducted an experimental study
focusing on the effects of communication skills education on university students aged 18–24, with an average age of 19.47.
The study emphasized the importance of communication in healthcare, professional, and social settings and revealed that
andragogy-based training significantly enhanced the students' empathetic abilities and emotional expression.
These results show that young adults represent a key developmental group for communication training, as improvements
in this stage can lead to measurable gains in functional communication, which is essential for addressing life’s
interpersonal demands and challenges.

Clegg (2012), on the other hand, applied dialogical narrative phenomenology to analyze 16 socially awkward
experiences. The study identified social awkwardness as a subjective experience rooted in perceived social or moral
transgressions, often triggering heightened social consciousness. Participants described awkwardness as both uncomfortable
and transformative—either avoiding such encounters or confronting them directly. These insights underscore how awkward
moments may cultivate social awareness and change. For chatbot design, such findings are valuable, as they provide a
framework for recognizing socially awkward cues (e.g., off-target speech or norm violations), interpreting user behavior
(e.g., evasiveness or anxiety), and deploying appropriate resolution strategies (e.g., avoidance or confrontation).
Ultimately, the study suggests that awkwardness, when properly understood, can be leveraged to promote healthier social 
relationships.

Furthermore, now looking into the experiences of neurodivergent individuals, Dunn et al. (2023) investigated differences
in social trait preferences between autistic and non-autistic adults. Involving 24 participants from each group, the study
showed that autistic adults viewed themselves as more socially awkward and less competent but reported a preference for
interacting with others who shared similar social traits. These findings challenge the notion that traditional or
“neurotypical” communication styles should be universally adopted. Instead, they highlight the need for broader tolerance
and acceptance of diverse communicative modes. For chatbot design and social communication tools, this implies a
shift toward more inclusive interaction models that account for varied social expectations and comfort levels across 
user groups.

\textbf{2.5} \textbf{Comparison of Large Language Models as Conversational AI}

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence, more specifically large language models, have enabled the making of conversational agents that can support both emotional and educational needs.  This technology is continuously being explored due to its potential as an effective solution because of advantages such as being accessible.  With the amount of LLMs available, it is important to choose the correct and appropriate model to suit the needs and purpose of the chatbot.
A Gemini AI-powered virtual companion named ELEVATE was introduced by Angeline et al. (2024) that was designed to be a mental health support provider for students who are focusing on preparing for high-stakes examinations. This virtual companion utilizes Natural Language Processing (NLP), real-time sentiment analysis, motivational support, and customized study plans for personalized guidance tailored to the different needs that students may have. ELEVATE’s performance in terms of accuracy, empathy score, average response time, and user satisfaction, were measure across 5 iterations and it showed continuous improvement on all metrics over each iteration. During the first iteration, it was found that the companion needed improvement on contextual understanding and empathetic engagement. Iterations 2 and 3 focused on improvements in NLP and response time, and enhanced sentiment analysis and intent recognition respectively, which led to increased accuracy and empathy scores. The fourth and fifth iterations focused on response and contextual depth refinement while further increasing the accuracy score, empathy score, and decreasing average response time which led to the study’s peak user satisfaction at 4.7 out of 5 by the fifth iteration. As such, these results show the accessibility and effectiveness of Gemini-based virtual companions, and that Gemini is a viable candidate as the foundational LLM for our social communications chatbot.

OpenAI’s GPT models, such as GPT-2, GPT-3, GPT-3.5, and GPT-4 have led to significant developments in natural language understanding, especially in the areas of social communication and pragmatic competence. These models are trained on massive datasets and can generate coherent and contextually appropriate language. However, their capabilities differ significantly depending on the version, particularly in their ability to understand implied meanings, adapt to conversation norms, and simulate human communication.

Two task-oriented dialogue chatbots were developed by Lee et al. (2024) in order to investigate their capabilities in teaching English to 22 undergraduate students in a private university in Seoul, South Korea who are either majoring in English or general education. These chatbots were made using PoeAI with ChatGPT as its underlying model, and prompts were added to fine-tune the bot into its role was and how it was expected to respond to user prompts. The first chatbot, AirlineTicketingBot, made use of a professional tone to simulate purchasing an airline ticket. The second bot, InsomniaBot, adopted a more casual, friendly, and empathetic approach and was designed to help users who are looking for advice about insomnia by giving them suggestions on how to sleep better. This study showed that participants had a positive perception towards the GPT-based chatbots, with feedback such as the bots feeling authentic and like talking to a native speaker, due to its ability to understand users’ intentions and respond appropriately even if the users had some typographical or grammatical errors. The capabilities mentioned build a good case for GPT to also be considered due to its to serve as a natural and flexible conversational agent that can be used in training for social situations. Its ability to adapt to different roles, also makes GPT a suitable candidate for use as both an AI Mentor with a professional role and an AI Partner with a casual role.

Bojic et al. (2023) conducted an empirical study comparing the pragmatic abilities of different LLMs using 20 dialogue tasks grounded in Grice’s maxims of conversation. For instance, short dialogues between two people were constructed to include subtle communication, i.e. sarcasm, irony, or an indirect response, which disregarded or violated at least of the Grice's maxims. In this case, the participant was asked how they would interpret the given dialogue - what one person really means, why one person responded a certain way, or what the implied meaning in the conversation was. As such, the tasks tested each model’s understanding of implicatures, indirect language, and relevance.  The results showed a clear hierarchy of performance: GPT-4 scored the highest with an average rating of 4.8 out of 5, outperforming not only earlier models (like GPT-2 and GPT-3) but also human participants. GPT-2 ranked the lowest, indicating that earlier models still struggled with interpreting subtle pragmatic cues. In addition to this, GPT-4 also completed the tasks the fastest, suggesting it was not only more accurate but more efficient as well.

Despite this, some limitations still remain. Eragamreddy (2025) observed that while AI systems are increasingly able to respond to explicit communication, they often fall short when faced with indirectness, ambiguity, or cultural nuance. This caused some concerns to be raised in which users may unconsciously adjust their communication style to match the limitations of AI, potentially weakening their own pragmatic skills in the process. Users often simplify their language, avoid complex metaphors or sarcasm, and adapt to "machine-friendly" communication patterns. Furthermore, while GPT-4 can simulate some aspects of human conversation, such as responding appropriately to sarcasm or irony, it still lacks the ability to interpret non-verbal cues, emotional undertones, or culture, which are essential for real-life communication - more particularly in sensitive or emotionally charged conversations.

\textbf{2.5.1 Evaluating Implicature in Large Language Models}

A component of effective social communication can be observed in one's ability to understand and produce \textit{implicature}, which is essentially the implied meanings conveyed through indirect or contextually nuanced language. This capability, in pragmatics, is essential for developing conversational agents that are sensitive to social norms, subtle cues, and the dynamics of real-world conversations. Although Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated notable progress in areas such as syntax, semantics, and general dialogue, their ability in catching implicatures continues to face limitations.

Anuranjana et al.\ \cite{anuranjana2024implicature} conducted a survey of recent computational approaches to implicature, underlining key datasets developed for its evaluation, including CIRCA, GRICE, IMPRESS, and CircaPlus. Given this, these benchmarks assess an LLM's capacity to infer implied meanings from context, indirect responses, and conversational maxims. Although models like GPT-4 have shown improved performance, achieving 81.8\% accuracy on conversational implicature datasets, they still fall short of human-level understanding, which typically exceeds 93\% on comparable tasks. On the other hand, instruction-tuned models, such as FlanT5-XXL, performed well on reasoning tasks (e.g., 82.9\% on GRICE), implying that fine-tuning for specific pragmatic objectives can improve their capabilities.

However, the study also discusses the persistent challenges that emerged. Many LLMs struggle with context-dependent inference, particularly when interpreting indirect language or culturally sensitive cues. Additionally, the existing implicature datasets are considerably smaller (e.g., typically 2.5k samples per task) compared to other benchmarks, limiting the models' exposure to varied conversational contexts.
