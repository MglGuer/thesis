\textbf{2.5} \textbf{Comparison of Large Language Models as Conversational AI}

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence, more specifically large language models, have enabled the making of conversational agents that can support both emotional and educational needs.  This technology is continuously being explored due to its potential as an effective solution because of advantages such as being accessible.  With the amount of LLMs available, it is important to choose the correct and appropriate model to suit the needs and purpose of the chatbot.
A Gemini AI-powered virtual companion named ELEVATE was introduced by Angeline et al. (2024) that was designed to be a mental health support provider for students who are focusing on preparing for high-stakes examinations. This virtual companion utilizes Natural Language Processing (NLP), real-time sentiment analysis, motivational support, and customized study plans for personalized guidance tailored to the different needs that students may have. ELEVATE’s performance in terms of accuracy, empathy score, average response time, and user satisfaction, were measure across 5 iterations and it showed continuous improvement on all metrics over each iteration. During the first iteration, it was found that the companion needed improvement on contextual understanding and empathetic engagement. Iterations 2 and 3 focused on improvements in NLP and response time, and enhanced sentiment analysis and intent recognition respectively, which led to increased accuracy and empathy scores. The fourth and fifth iterations focused on response and contextual depth refinement while further increasing the accuracy score, empathy score, and decreasing average response time which led to the study’s peak user satisfaction at 4.7 out of 5 by the fifth iteration. As such, these results show the accessibility and effectiveness of Gemini-based virtual companions, and that Gemini is a viable candidate as the foundational LLM for our social communications chatbot.

OpenAI’s GPT models, such as GPT-2, GPT-3, GPT-3.5, and GPT-4 have led to significant developments in natural language understanding, especially in the areas of social communication and pragmatic competence. These models are trained on massive datasets and can generate coherent and contextually appropriate language. However, their capabilities differ significantly depending on the version, particularly in their ability to understand implied meanings, adapt to conversation norms, and simulate human communication.

Two task-oriented dialogue chatbots were developed by Lee et al. (2024) in order to investigate their capabilities in teaching English to 22 undergraduate students in a private university in Seoul, South Korea who are either majoring in English or general education. These chatbots were made using PoeAI with ChatGPT as its underlying model, and prompts were added to fine-tune the bot into its role was and how it was expected to respond to user prompts. The first chatbot, AirlineTicketingBot, made use of a professional tone to simulate purchasing an airline ticket. The second bot, InsomniaBot, adopted a more casual, friendly, and empathetic approach and was designed to help users who are looking for advice about insomnia by giving them suggestions on how to sleep better. This study showed that participants had a positive perception towards the GPT-based chatbots, with feedback such as the bots feeling authentic and like talking to a native speaker, due to its ability to understand users’ intentions and respond appropriately even if the users had some typographical or grammatical errors. The capabilities mentioned build a good case for GPT to also be considered due to its to serve as a natural and flexible conversational agent that can be used in training for social situations. Its ability to adapt to different roles, also makes GPT a suitable candidate for use as both an AI Mentor with a professional role and an AI Partner with a casual role.

Bojic et al. (2023) conducted an empirical study comparing the pragmatic abilities of different LLMs using 20 dialogue tasks grounded in Grice’s maxims of conversation. For instance, short dialogues between two people were constructed to include subtle communication, i.e. sarcasm, irony, or an indirect response, which disregarded or violated at least of the Grice's maxims. In this case, the participant was asked how they would interpret the given dialogue - what one person really means, why one person responded a certain way, or what the implied meaning in the conversation was. As such, the tasks tested each model’s understanding of implicatures, indirect language, and relevance.  The results showed a clear hierarchy of performance: GPT-4 scored the highest with an average rating of 4.8 out of 5, outperforming not only earlier models (like GPT-2 and GPT-3) but also human participants. GPT-2 ranked the lowest, indicating that earlier models still struggled with interpreting subtle pragmatic cues. In addition to this, GPT-4 also completed the tasks the fastest, suggesting it was not only more accurate but more efficient as well.

Despite this, some limitations still remain. Eragamreddy (2025) observed that while AI systems are increasingly able to respond to explicit communication, they often fall short when faced with indirectness, ambiguity, or cultural nuance. This caused some concerns to be raised in which users may unconsciously adjust their communication style to match the limitations of AI, potentially weakening their own pragmatic skills in the process. Users often simplify their language, avoid complex metaphors or sarcasm, and adapt to "machine-friendly" communication patterns. Furthermore, while GPT-4 can simulate some aspects of human conversation, such as responding appropriately to sarcasm or irony, it still lacks the ability to interpret non-verbal cues, emotional undertones, or culture, which are essential for real-life communication - more particularly in sensitive or emotionally charged conversations.
