\section{Grice's Maxims in Pragmatic Analysis}

Conversational implicature, a core concept in pragmatics, refers to the unstated meaning conveyed in an utterance that goes beyond its literal interpretation (Eragamreddy, 2025). It is a meaning that is implied by a speaker and inferred by a listener, based on shared understanding of conversational principles, particularly Grice's Cooperative Principle and its associated maxims (Yeboah, 2021 \& Levinson 1983, 2013 as cited in Eragamreddy, 2025). Implicatures arise when speakers intentionally or unintentionally deviate from these maxims, prompting the listener to infer an unstated meaning to maintain the assumption of cooperativeness. In the context of this research, pragmatic competence is defined as the user's ability to navigate the tension between the literal words spoken and the intended social message.

Moreover, this is utilized by both chatbot and user. This in turn allows the understanding of a chatbot's ability to simulate realistic conversation and competence in understanding implied meaning all-the-while utilizing this in response generation for the user to practice pragmatics. Central to this process is the concept of implicature flouting, where a speaker blatantly violates a maxim to trigger an inference. When an interlocutor appears to disregard a conversational maxim (Quantity, Quality, Relation, or Manner), it prompts the listener to infer an alternative meaning that aligns with the assumption of cooperative communication. In the context of our chatbot, the AI Partner and AI Mentor take into consideration these intentional maxims violations to evaluate how well a user navigates implied meaning.

One of the foundational theories underpinning the study of pragmatics is H. Paul Grice's theory of conversational implicature, introduced in his seminal work Logic and Conversation (1975). Grice proposed that effective communication relies not only on the literal meaning of utterances, but also on shared assumptions between speaker and listener about how communication should proceed. This principle is guided by 4 maxims that speakers are expected to follow in conversation: Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner. These maxims serve as a framework for analyzing conversational exchanges and understanding how meaning is constructed beyond the literal words spoken~\cite{zheng_grice_2021}.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Maxim of Quantity} \\
    This maxim states that speakers should provide the right amount of information: not too much, which can overwhelm or confuse, and not too little, which can leave the listener guessing. For example, if asked about a meeting time, a concise answer like "3 PM" is preferable to an overly detailed response that includes unnecessary background information.

    \item \textbf{Maxim of Quality} \\
    This maxim emphasizes truthfulness and accuracy in communication. Speakers should not say what they believe to be false or for which they lack sufficient evidence. For instance, if a speaker claims to have completed a task when they have not, they violate this maxim.

    \item \textbf{Maxim of Relation} \\
    This maxim requires that contributions to a conversation be relevant to the topic at hand. Irrelevant responses can lead to confusion or miscommunication. For example, if discussing weekend plans and someone suddenly talks about their favorite movie, it violates this maxim.

    \item \textbf{Maxim of Manner} \\
    This maxim calls for clarity and orderliness in communication. Speakers should avoid ambiguity and obscurity, ensuring their message is easy to understand. For instance, using clear language and logical structure helps adhere to this maxim.
\end{enumerate}


When individuals intentionally violate these maxims, it is often an implicature; a meaning that is implied rather than explicitly stated. For instance, responding to a question: "How was the meeting?" with: "Well, the coffee was nice," gives off an implication that the meeting itself may not have been productive. In these cases, interpreting indirect or nuanced messages requires elaborate understanding of social context, cultural norms, and shared knowledge --- all of which are components of pragmatic competence.

Pragmatic competence refers to a speaker's ability to use and interpret language appropriately in social interactions. It comprises not only the knowledge of linguistics but also the ability to infer meaning based on context, recognize implicatures, and handling of conversational norms. As such, understanding Grice's Maxims is essential for developing pragmatic competence, due to the fact that it enables individuals to infer implied meanings, respond appropriately in various conversational settings, and use language appropriately.

In this context, Grice's Maxims serve as a theoretical foundation for designing chatbot interactions that simulate real-life conversational settings. By exposing the users to simulated conversations wherein maxims are observed or violated, they are given the opportunity to reflect on meaning beyond the literal, practice inferential reasoning, and make improvements to their ability to navigate social communication. In this fashion, the development of the users' pragmatic competence, especially amongst those who may struggle with social communication, is cultivated~\cite{yang_social_2024}.



\section{The APAM Framework: Dual-Agent Structure}

While Grice's Maxims provide an outline for the particular aspects of social interaction a chatbot might be able to address, the APAM Framework provides the structural approach for effective delivery of this support. This framework uses a dual-agent approach for the purposes of enhancing the ability of chatbots to simulate realistic scenarios while also delivering grounded, knowledgeable feedback~\cite{yang_social_2024}. Finally, it offers users a safe, supportive environment in which they can learn social communication skills. Thus, the functionality of the chatbot will be divided according to the two:

\subsection{AI Partner: Realistic Practice}

The AI Partner is critical for the Scenario Selection and Conversation Simulation phases, acting as a second person for the user's simulated conversation. Its focuses are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Realism}: Creating convincing and diverse situations, especially those for which Grice's Maxims would be relevant.
    \item \textbf{Consistency}: Portraying a personality consistent with, and plausible for the nature of the simulated situation.
    \item \textbf{Structure}: A gradual increase in scenario difficulty, helping users apply pragmatics in increasingly complex and realistic scenarios.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{AI Mentor: Personalized Feedback}

The AI Mentor, in this research, would target the Evaluation and Reflection or Retry Phases by giving theory-based, knowledgeable comments suited to the user as well as to their performance. Its focus areas are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adaptation}: Providing tailored feedback based on how the user did, and providing insights into their strengths and weaknesses per situation.
    \item \textbf{Knowledge}: Making use of existing theoretical frameworks (in this case Grice's Maxims), to expound on any missteps or good conversational moves the user makes.
    \item \textbf{Empathy}: Being constructive, first and foremost, and avoiding being unnecessarily critical, and being respectful in giving feedback.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scenario Design: Simulating Realistic Conversations}
The design of conversational scenarios is a critical aspect of the chatbot's functionality, as it directly impacts the user's ability to practice and develop pragmatic 
competence. The scenarios will be crafted to reflect real-life situations where Grice's Maxims are relevant, such as social gatherings, workplace interactions, 
or casual conversations. Each scenario will be designed with varying levels of complexity, allowing users to gradually build their skills in interpreting and responding to 
conversational cues. By simulating realistic conversations, the chatbot can provide users with a safe and supportive environment to practice their social communication skills, 
receive feedback, and ultimately enhance their pragmatic competence. In order to decide the appropriateness of the scenarios, they must follow the following theories:

\subsection{Havighurst's Developmental Task Theory}
Havighurst's Developmental Task Theory posits the concept of "teachable moments." Individuals face specific developmental tasks at different stages of life, which are 
influenced by biological, psychological, and social factors. For young adults aged 15-24, key developmental tasks include forming intimate relationships, establishing a 
career path, and developing a sense of identity. When designing scenarios for the chatbot, it is essential to incorporate situations that reflect these developmental tasks. 
For example, scenarios could involve navigating social interactions in a workplace setting, managing conflicts in personal relationships, or making decisions that impact one's 
future. By aligning the scenarios with Havighurst's theory, the chatbot can provide relevant and meaningful practice opportunities for users to develop their pragmatic 
competence in contexts that are pertinent to their stage of life.

\subsection{Arnett's Theory of Emerging Adulthood}
Arnett's Theory of Emerging Adulthood states that young people do not directly jump from adolescence to young adulthood. Instead, it identifies the period from late teens 
to mid-twenties, around 18 to 29 years old, as a distinct developmental stage characterized by exploration and instability. During this time, individuals are exploring their 
identity, values, and future goals. This stage is marked by a sense of instability and self-focus, as young adults navigate the transition from adolescence to adulthood. 
The chatbot's scenarios should reflect this exploration phase by presenting situations that encourage users to reflect on their values and aspirations, while also providing 
support in navigating the complexities of emerging adulthood.

\subsection{Filipino Cultural Values}
Conversational context and pragmatics are deeply influenced by cultural norms and values. Linguistic choices and the maxims' meaning and influence in one culture can be perceived as abrasive or inappropriate in another. To ground this, the Philippine context is utilized in the chatbot. Communication considering Filipino culture and values such as \textit{Pakikisama} (social harmony), \textit{Paggalang} (respect for elders), and \textit{Hiya} (sense of shame) will be integrated into the scenarios.
This ensures that the chatbot's interactions are culturally relevant and resonate with the target demographic, allowing users to practice pragmatic competence in a context that reflects their real-life social interactions. This also limits the demographic to primarily Filipino users.


\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Pakikisama (Social Harmony):} Prioritizing interpersonal cohesion and cooperation by avoiding direct confrontation or conflict.
\item \textbf{Hiya (Propriety):} A sense of social propriety that regulates behavior to prevent bringing shame or dishonor to oneself or one's family.
\item \textbf{Utang na Loob (Reciprocal Debt):} A deep sense of gratitude and the moral obligation to honor social and familial commitments.
\item \textbf{Kapwa (Shared Identity):} The recognition of a shared inner self, treating others as equals and fellow members of a community.
\item \textbf{Paggalang (Deference):} The practice of showing explicit respect, particularly toward elders and those in positions of authority.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Synthesized Theories of Arnett and Havighurst: Four Defined Themes}
This research then synthesizes the theories of Havighurst and Arnett to guide the AI's analysis and the scenario design to spearhead the chatbot's functionality. While Grice’s
Maxims provide the linguistic rules, Havighurst and Arnett provide the developmental "stakes" of the conversation. These are further filtered through Filipino Cultural Values 
(such as \textit{Pakikisama} and \textit{Hiya}) to ensure the scenarios resonate with the target demographic. This synthesis ensures that the chatbot has theoretical grounding
in both the linguistic and developmental aspects of social interactions in a culturally-focused manner. The four themes are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Theme 1: Identity vs. Expectation (Authenticity vs. Obligation)} \\
    This theme is built on the collision between a young adult’s emerging sense of self \cite{arnett_emerging_2000} and the life path prescribed for them by family or society \cite{havighurst_developmental_1948}. In the Filipino context, this often manifests as a tension between personal desire and \textit{Utang na Loob} (debt of gratitude) or \textit{Paggalang} (respect for elders).
    \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conflict:} The user’s personal goals, beliefs, or identity clash with the external expectations of an authority figure.
    \item \textbf{Formula:} The user develops a personal goal or identity $\rightarrow$ An authority figure expresses a conflicting expectation or "helpful" advice $\rightarrow$ The user is forced to defend, explain, or conceal their choice.
    \item \textbf{Goal:} To practice validating the authority’s perspective and expressing gratitude while respectfully asserting one's own autonomy.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Theme 2: Autonomy vs. Dependence (Independence vs. Hierarchy)} \\
    This theme explores the frustration of the "in-between" stage \cite{arnett_emerging_2000}, where the user seeks adult responsibilities \cite{havighurst_developmental_1948} but is still treated as a dependent. It addresses the transition from a vertical hierarchy to an adult-to-adult relationship.
    \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conflict:} The user’s attempt to exercise adult autonomy is challenged by a figure who views them as incapable or dependent.
    \item \textbf{Formula:} The user makes an adult decision (e.g., financial, travel, or relationship) $\rightarrow$ A guardian or older sibling questions or overrules the decision $\rightarrow$ The user must justify their competence without appearing childishly defiant.
    \item \textbf{Goal:} To shift the conversation from "obedience" to "responsibility," demonstrating reasoning and competence to re-evaluate the relationship as adult-to-adult.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Theme 3: Ambiguity vs. Definition (Clarity vs. Fear of Loss)} \\
    This theme addresses the instability of relationships during emerging adulthood \cite{arnett_emerging_2000} while attempting to achieve the developmental task of forming stable, intimate partnerships \cite{havighurst_developmental_1948}. It highlights the tension between the need for Gricean clarity and the fear of losing \textit{Pakikisama} (social harmony).
    \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conflict:} A relationship exists in an undefined "gray area," and a specific event forces the need for clarity.
    \item \textbf{Formula:} The user is in an ambiguous relationship (e.g., a "situationship") $\rightarrow$ A catalyst occurs (e.g., a holiday or a third party) $\rightarrow$ The user must initiate or respond to a "Define the Relationship" (DTR) conversation.
    \item \textbf{Goal:} To practice expressing feelings and uncertainties with Gricean Quality and Manner, creating a safe space for honesty even when the outcome is uncertain.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Theme 4: Group vs. Self (Harmony vs. Integrity)} \\
    This theme focuses on the formation of a personal value system \cite{havighurst_developmental_1948} when it conflicts with the established norms of a peer group, a central part of identity exploration \cite{arnett_emerging_2000}. It navigates the pressure of \textit{Pakikisama} against the value of personal integrity.
    \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conflict:} The user’s personal values are violated by the actions or words of their peers.
    \item \textbf{Formula:} A peer group engages in unethical or cruel behavior (e.g., gossiping or bullying) $\rightarrow$ The user’s silence implies agreement $\rightarrow$ The user must choose between staying silent or intervening.
    \item \textbf{Goal:} To practice intervening in a way that minimizes defensiveness, using redirection or questioning to uphold personal integrity while de-escalating conflict.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

These were operationalized to specific formulas in order to guide both system and user. The system guidance is through the proper scenario generation and roleplay aspect, and the user guidance is through specific goal achievement and reflection on the conversation. 
Through this, the chatbot has structured presentation of the theoretical frameworks and a clear path for the user to follow in order to practice and develop their pragmatical ability.

\section{Pragmatic Competence Scoring Framework}
To evaluate the user's mastery over the rules and theories as mentioned above, the research adopts a framework introduced by Sileo et al. (2022) to categorize and measure pragmatic performance. This was a framework developed to the address the limitations of Natural Language Understanding (NLU) benchmarks (such as GLUE)
which focuses on semantic content. The framework had introduced a rudimentary taxonomy of three groups (Groups A, B, and C) which is then utilized and adjusted to be operationalized for Socius. These groups and adjustments are the following:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Group A: Intention and Structural Coherence} 
    Sileo identifies this as the layer prioritizing communicative intent, which relates the communicative action that the user is trying to achieve (such as requesting, refusing, or apologizing) and how that action and intent relates to the conversation logically. 
    Experimentally, this was demonstrated by showing that AI models often struggled to identify these underlying goals, which leads to a breakdown in the logical flow of a dialogue. In the context of Socius, this formed the basis for the category \textbf{Intention Coherence}, which allows 
    the AI mentor to evaluate the user's "speech act" and if it is consistent and aligned to the current situation/conversation.
    \item \textbf{Group B: Social, Affective, and Contextual Nuance}
    This group covers the interpersonal variables such as politeness, emotional tone, and level of detail that defines the current social setting of a conversation. This group was identified through a "Human-Model Gap" exposed by AI frequently failing to match human judgment when it comes to subtle social cues and formality.
    This was then the basis for the category \textbf{Social \& Affective Nuance}, which assesses the user's ability to understand social cues through cultural markers and proper formality level. 
    \item \textbf{Group C: Functional Success}
    This group focuses on an outcome based evaluation, wherein it measures if the user's speech act had achieved their intended goal (in communication). Sileo found that the models understood the literal meaning of a sentence but they could not understand whether it was a realistic, persuasive, and successful response in a real-world social interaction.
    This became the basis for the category \textbf{Communicative Effectiveness}, measuring whether the user's strategy had succesfully resolved the scenario's conflict without damaging the relationship.    
\end{itemize}

Thus, synthesizing the high-level taxonomy of Sileo et al. (2022) and the specific goals of the chatbot, the research required a granular scoring rubric to define a quantitative assessment of the user's performance. This rubric below utilizes eight criteria across the three categories mentioned above, which is scored on a 5-point likert scale:

The rubric above was designed by the researchers to be used by the AI mentor in evaluating the user's performance in the simulated conversations. The AI mentor would base it's feedback on the defined submetrics and give a score for each, collectively showing the score of the different categories to the user after the conversation. The submetrics are given scores
and the mean between the submetrics of each category is calculated to give the category score. This was evaluated by the expert panel to ensure that the rubric is valid and reliable for the purposes of this research. The rubric allows the chatbot to give specific, actionable feedback to the user, guiding them on how to improve their pragmatic competence in future interactions.
